# Racing Pigeon Salmonella (Paratyphoid)

> **Short answer**: Pigeon paratyphoid is caused by *Salmonella enterica* (most often *S.* Typhimurium in pigeons) and carries a triple threat: it **persists in carriers**, it **transmits vertically through the egg**, and it **infects humans**. The CDC estimates *Salmonella* causes roughly **1.35 million illnesses, 26,500 hospitalizations and 420 deaths** in the US each year alone — and a documented Iranian racing-pigeon outbreak killed **21% of the flock**.

**Salmonella** infection, called "paratyphoid" in pigeons, is a common bacterial disease and also a **zoonotic pathogen** — it can infect humans. It is not one disease with one face: *Salmonella enterica* encompasses **more than 2,500 serovars**, and the ones that matter to pigeons are primarily host-adapted *S.* Typhimurium strains, with *S.* Enteritidis also implicated. That diversity is why paratyphoid presents so variably and why laboratory identification matters.

## Pathogen Characteristics

*Salmonella* is a Gram-negative rod-shaped bacterium with considerable environmental resistance — it can survive for long periods in the loft environment, particularly in moist, organic-rich conditions. In racing pigeons, infection often follows a **chronic, insidious course**, making it one of the "invisible" pathogens of the loft. Carriers may shed the organism intermittently for months while appearing outwardly normal, which is precisely why subclinical infection is so dangerous: it spreads quietly through the flock before obvious disease appears.

Recent genomic work has clarified what "pigeon paratyphoid" actually is at the strain level. A 2021 investigation of three pigeon paratyphoid outbreaks in Henan, China isolated 53 pigeon-origin *S.* Typhimurium (STM) strains, finding they belonged to distinct host-adapted clones (ST128-DT2, ST19-DT99) — and suggested intercontinental spread via pigeon eggs ([PLOS Pathogens](https://journals.plos.org/plospathogens/article?id=10.1371/journal.ppat.1012992)). That is a concrete warning: pigeon-adapted *Salmonella* is not a static local problem; it moves through the egg trade.

## Clinical Signs

Pigeon paratyphoid presents with highly variable signs, commonly including:

- **Articular form**: swollen wing and leg joints, lameness, reduced flying ability
- **Enteric form**: persistent diarrhea, weight loss, ruffled feathers
- **Neurological form**: head tilt, loss of balance
- **Reproductive**: reduced egg production, weak squabs
- **"Black belly"**: pigeon-adapted STM infection in squabs can present with a darkened abdomen (necrotizing enteritis), as documented in the Henan outbreaks

Because these signs overlap with many other pigeon diseases, laboratory confirmation is essential for an accurate diagnosis.

## How Serious Is It? The Numbers

- **Zoonotic burden**: the CDC estimates *Salmonella* causes approximately **1.35 million illnesses, 26,500 hospitalizations and 420 deaths** annually in the US alone ([CDC, cited in PLOS Pathogens](https://journals.plos.org/plospathogens/article?id=10.1371/journal.ppat.1012992)). Pigeon contact is one of many routes, but the zoonotic potential is real and documented.
- **Flock mortality**: a 2023 case report of a homing-pigeon flock in Iran described non-specific signs (diarrhea, weight loss, weakness, drooping wings) with a **21.05% mortality rate**, and necropsy confirmed severe necrotic hemorrhagic enteritis ([Iran study](https://jzd.tabrizu.ac.ir/article_10587.html)).
- **Vertical transmission**: infected breeders can transmit the organism through the egg, so a single carrier can seed infection across an entire breeding season.

## Zoonotic Risk

Salmonella can infect humans through contact with sick birds or contaminated environments, causing fever, diarrhea and other symptoms. Practical precautions:

- Always wash hands and disinfect after handling sick birds
- Keep the loft clean and well ventilated
- Immunocompromised individuals should avoid contact with sick birds

## Diagnosis & Detection

Conventional bacterial culture is slow and relatively insensitive. [Sanshi Bio virus detection](/en/virus-detection/) uses **real-time PCR** for fast, precise Salmonella detection within hours. Molecular methods also allow strain-level resolution, which matters for understanding whether you are dealing with a host-adapted pigeon strain and where it came from.

## Prevention & Control Measures

1. **Quarantine new birds** — isolate and test every new pigeon before introduction
2. **Breeder screening** — test breeding stock before the breeding season to block vertical transmission
3. **Environmental management** — keep the loft dry and disinfect regularly
4. **Prompt treatment** — treat confirmed cases under veterinary guidance, guided by sensitivity testing

> 💡 Tip: Salmonella can persist long-term in carriers. Combine routine [pathogen testing](/en/virus-detection/) with ongoing health monitoring.

## Control at the Source

Because Salmonella combines environmental persistence, a chronic carrier state and zoonotic potential, it cannot be managed by treatment alone. A layered strategy is required: quarantine and test every new bird, screen breeding stock before the season to block vertical transmission, keep housing dry and disinfected, and rely on rapid real-time PCR for early diagnosis. This breaks the transmission chain at its weakest links and protects both the loft and the people who work in it.

## The Author's Take

**Position**: In my view, the fancier's biggest Salmonella mistake is treating it as a "sick bird" problem when it is really a "carrier and egg" problem — the disease you can see is the tip of the iceberg.

**Reasoning**: First, carriers shed intermittently for months while looking normal, so visual inspection misses the reservoir. Second, vertical transmission through the egg means one undetected breeder contaminates an entire season — the Henan strain data showing international spread via eggs makes this concrete, not theoretical. Third, the 21% Iranian mortality and the CDC's zoonotic burden figures mean the downside is not a few days of loose droppings; it is flock losses plus a human-health liability. Test breeders and new birds, and the iceberg shrinks.

**Disclosure**: This is my editorial judgment based on published epidemiology and outbreak reports, not a claim that every loft harbors carrier birds. The only way to know your flock's true status is testing.

## FAQ

### Can Salmonella infect humans?
Yes. It is zoonotic — the CDC attributes roughly 1.35 million annual illnesses in the US to *Salmonella* ([CDC](https://journals.plos.org/plospathogens/article?id=10.1371/journal.ppat.1012992)). Practice hygiene after handling birds and keep the loft clean.

### Can Salmonella infection be cured?
Proper antibiotics can control it, but follow the full course and do sensitivity testing to avoid resistance. Breeder purification is the key to eradication.

### Can Salmonella be eradicated from the flock?
With veterinary-guided treatment the infection can be controlled, but Salmonella can persist long-term in carriers and frequently recurs. Combine routine [pathogen testing](/en/virus-detection/) with pre-breeding screening of breeding stock.

### How deadly is pigeon paratyphoid?
Mortality varies by strain and flock condition; one documented homing-pigeon outbreak reached a **21.05% mortality rate** ([Iran study](https://jzd.tabrizu.ac.ir/article_10587.html)). Chronic carrier infections are more common than explosive mortality in well-managed lofts.

### What is the difference between paratyphoid and typhoid fever?
Paratyphoid is caused by various *Salmonella* serovars and produces relatively diverse signs; both are *Salmonella* infections, and laboratory testing identifies the specific pathogen. Pigeon paratyphoid is typically caused by host-adapted *S.* Typhimurium strains.

### How can Salmonella infection be prevented?
Maintain loft hygiene, keep feed and drinking water clean, disinfect regularly, isolate and test new birds, and screen breeders before the breeding season. See the [prevention guide](/en/blog/salmonella-prevention/) for the full four-step program.

### Which sample for Salmonella?
Cloacal swabs or feces; tissue samples may be used for joint or neurological signs.

## Key Takeaways

1. A zoonotic pathogen — practice hygiene when handling sick birds; CDC: 1.35M US illnesses/year.
2. Vertical transmission risk — infected breeders can pass it via eggs, seeding an entire season.
3. Diverse symptoms — digestive, joint and neurological signs; squabs may show "black belly."
4. PCR enables early diagnosis — faster and more sensitive than culture.
5. Documented flock mortality of 21% in one outbreak — not a trivial disease.
6. Breeder purification + disinfection — control it at the source.
