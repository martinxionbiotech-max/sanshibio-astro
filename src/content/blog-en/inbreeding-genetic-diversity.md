**Short answer:** “Pure” is not the goal of pigeon breeding. Inbreeding can stabilise some traits, but it also raises the chance that harmful recessive variants meet, and it can reduce fertility, immunity and overall robustness. Manage diversity, not just repeated names in a pedigree.

## 1. What inbreeding actually does

An inbreeding coefficient describes the probability that two alleles at a locus come from a common ancestor. In plain terms: the closer the parents, the greater the chance that the offspring inherits the same ancestral segment twice.

It is not a good/bad switch:

- moderate inbreeding can fix a useful combination
- excessive inbreeding makes harmful recessives more likely to meet
- the effect differs by strain and trait

## 2. The reasoning: why “purer is better” is a dangerous shortcut

### Homozygosity fixes both advantages and defects

Inbreeding increases homozygosity. That can make a favourable trait more consistent, but it can also make a harmful recessive more visible. The pedigree looks stable; the risk may be stable too.

### Small populations amplify drift

When the effective breeding population is small, some bloodlines become concentrated over generations even if each selection looks reasonable. Diversity can fall without anyone deliberately choosing “too close.”

### Race performance can hide breeding problems

A successful racer may still carry a recessive burden affecting fertility or immunity. Selecting only on race results confuses racing value with breeding value.

## 3. What follows: an inbreeding checklist

| Check | Question | Warning sign |
|---|---|---|
| Common ancestors | How often do they appear within 3–4 generations? | Repeated duplication |
| Effective population | How many birds actually breed? | A few sires dominate |
| Offspring health | Are weak chicks, fertility or immune issues repeating? | Same problem in the same pair |
| Trait evidence | Is consistency improving with performance? | “Pure” with no performance data |
| Outcross plan | Is there a plan to introduce unrelated blood? | Closed population indefinitely |
| Records | Can pairings and offspring be traced? | Memory and pedigree only |

## 4. Editorial take

I am not against inbreeding. I am against **unrecorded** inbreeding.

- A planned line can use a limited amount of inbreeding to fix a combination, but health, fertility and diversity must be watched.
- “Pure blood” without pedigree, offspring records and health data hides risk rather than managing it.
- Genetic testing can identify individual differences, but it cannot replace population-level breeding management.

If a close pair repeatedly produces weak chicks or health problems, I would not expand it because the bloodline is “pure.” I would introduce unrelated blood or reduce the inbreeding intensity.

## 5. Boundaries

This is a breeding-management framework, not a coefficient calculation or veterinary advice. The right level of inbreeding differs by strain; decisions should use pedigree, population size, health records and offspring performance.
