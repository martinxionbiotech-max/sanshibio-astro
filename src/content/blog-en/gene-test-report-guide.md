# How to Read a Pigeon Gene Test Report?

> **TL;DR**: Reading a pigeon gene test report comes down to "three steps" — first confirm the sample and items, then read the genotype and indicator interpretation, and finally land on the breeding advice. The critical mindset: **a genotype is a probability, not a prophecy** — it shows potential direction, while training, health and nutrition decide the actual result.

Holding a pigeon gene test report, many fanciers feel lost amid pages of "genotypes," "indicators" and "recommendations." How exactly do you read a **pigeon gene test report**? You only need to follow one thread — "from genotype to breeding advice" — to read and use the report well.

## The Four-Layer Structure of a Report

A complete Sanshi Bio test report is typically organized into four layers:

| Layer | Section | What to check |
|-------|---------|----------------|
| Layer 1 | Sample information | Pigeon ID, leg ring number and sampling date are correct |
| Layer 2 | Test items | Which genes/pathogens/relationships were tested |
| Layer 3 | Test results | The genotype at each locus, or positive/negative |
| Layer 4 | Conclusion & advice | Indicator interpretation and breeding/prevention advice |

**The first step is always to verify the sample information** — no matter how accurate the report, a wrong sample makes everything moot.

## The Core: How to Read the Genotype

Take the [flight ability gene test](/en/flight-ability/) report as an example. Each indicator gives the **genotype** of the corresponding gene:

| Indicator | Gene | What the genotype means |
|-----------|------|--------------------------|
| Endurance | LDHA | Lactic-acid metabolism; long-distance tolerance |
| Homing | DRD4a/b | Dopamine receptor; homing persistence |
| Navigation | CRY1 | Cryptochrome; directional precision |
| Muscle power | MSTN | Myostatin; muscle power and speed |
| Feather | F-KER | Keratin; feather quality |
| Memory | LRP8 | Synaptic plasticity; learning and memory |
| Bad-weather orientation | GSR | Oxidative stress defense; orientation in poor weather |
| Intelligence | CASK | Synaptic signaling; trainability and cognition |

**How to read the genotype**: the report labels each locus's genotype (e.g., AA, AG, GG) and gives a conclusion of "excellent / average / watch." Fanciers need not memorize genotype combinations — focus on the **conclusion and indicator interpretation**.

## Translating Genotype into Breeding Advice

Reading the genotype is only the first step; the key is putting the data to use. Take the endurance gene as an example:

- Strong endurance gene + average muscle gene → better suited to long-distance endurance races; when pairing, add a speed-type bloodline.
- Strong homing persistence + high navigation precision → suited to long-distance, complex-weather races.

**The translation logic**: genotype reflects "potential direction"; breeding advice combines "single-trait potential" into a "pairing plan." See the [breeding selection guide](/en/blog/breeding-selection-guide/).

## What a Genotype Can and Cannot Promise

This is the part fanciers most need to hear, and the part a report rarely states in bold: the eight flight-ability genes are **candidate genes** identified by association studies — meaning researchers found that certain variants *correlate* with racing performance, not that a "good" genotype *causes* a win.

The distinction is practical:

- **A favorable genotype raises the probability** of strong performance in that trait, and it is heritable — so it is real, useful information for pairing.
- **It does not guarantee results.** Racing performance is a product of genetics × training × health × nutrition × environment, and the genetic slice is itself polygenic — many genes each contribute a little. One "excellent" indicator is a nudge, not a destination.

The correct reading is therefore directional, never deterministic: treat the genotype as a *tiebreaker and pairing signal*, not a crystal ball. A loft that selects purely on genotype and ignores race results and condition will be outperformed by one that uses genotype to sharpen decisions it was already making.

## How to Read Other Report Types

Besides the flight ability report, two other report types are common:

| Report type | Core indicator | Key judgment |
|-------------|----------------|--------------|
| Paternity report | Paternity Index (PI) | Higher combined parentage probability means greater certainty |
| Pathogen report | Ct value | Lower Ct means higher load; 35–40 suggests re-testing |

Paternity reports are read by "parentage probability," pathogen reports by "positive/negative + Ct value" — different logic, don't mix them up.

For the paternity report, the number scales on a known ladder: a **combined paternity index (CPI)** of 99 → 99% probability, 999 → 99.9%, and 9,999 → **99.99%**. For the pathogen report, a Ct shift of roughly **3.3 cycles equals a 10-fold** change in viral load, and anything in the 35–40 range is a gray zone that warrants re-testing. See [report interpretation](/en/blog/test-report-interpretation/) for more.

## Three Common Mistakes When Reading Reports

1. **Reading a single indicator in isolation**: the 8 genes must be viewed together — one "average" indicator does not condemn the whole bird.
2. **Treating potential as results**: a good genotype ≠ guaranteed wins; training and health matter equally.
3. **Reading conclusions but skipping methods**: the testing method and quality-control notes are just as important — they determine the report's credibility.

## When to Ask for a Professional Read

The report's "excellent / average / watch" conclusions are designed to be self-service, but there are times when a human interpretation is worth more than re-reading the document:

- **Conflicting indicators** — e.g., strong endurance but weak muscle — where the breeding implication is not obvious.
- **A high-stakes decision** — importing a breeder, or committing a whole season's pairings — where a misread is expensive.
- **A pathogen result in the gray zone** — a Ct near the cut-off, or a paternity probability below the decisive range — where the trend matters more than the single number.

In these cases the value of the test is not in the page but in the *decision it unlocks*, and a specialist can translate the data into a concrete plan. The report is a starting point, not the endpoint — and knowing when to escalate is part of reading it well.

## FAQ

### How do I read a pigeon gene test report?
Follow three steps: verify the sample information, read the genotype and indicator interpretation, then land on the breeding advice — the "genotype → breeding advice" thread.

### What do genotypes like AA and AG mean?
They represent the allele combination at a locus; different combinations mean different ability tendencies. The report also gives an "excellent / average / watch" conclusion, so focus on that.

### Does a "good" genotype guarantee performance?
No. The flight-ability genes are candidate genes from association studies — they raise the *probability* of a trait, but racing results are shaped by training, health and nutrition too. Read the genotype as a direction, not a prophecy.

### If the report says "average" genotype, is the pigeon unusable?
No. One "average" indicator does not mean the pigeon is poor. Evaluate the 8 genes together with race results and health — never judge in isolation.

### How do I tell a flight ability report from a paternity report?
A flight ability report shows ability indicators per genotype; a paternity report shows parentage probability. Different purposes and indicators — see [report interpretation](/en/blog/test-report-interpretation/).

### What does a parentage probability of 99.99% correspond to?
A combined paternity index of roughly 10,000 — meaning the DNA evidence is about 10,000× more likely under the parent hypothesis than a random-bird hypothesis.

### Does the report give specific pairing advice?

## Key Takeaways

1. The report has four layers — sample, items, results and advice; verify the sample before reading results.
2. Read the genotype via the conclusion — reports give "excellent/average/watch" conclusions; no need to memorize combinations.
3. Translate genotype into breeding advice — combining single-trait potential into a pairing plan is the goal.
4. Don't read indicators in isolation — the 8 genes must be evaluated together.
5. Potential ≠ results — genotype is a probability, not a prophecy; training and health decide final performance.
