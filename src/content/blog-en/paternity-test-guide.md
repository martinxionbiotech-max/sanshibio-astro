> **TL;DR**: A pigeon paternity test compares alleles across 58 SNP loci to produce a **combined parentage probability of 99.99% or higher** when the evidence is decisive — but that number is a population statistic, not a literal "99.99% accuracy" guarantee. This guide explains the science, the math and where the limits actually are.

In pigeon trade and breeding, "bloodline" is the core of value. Traditional leg rings and pedigree papers cannot fully prevent fraud; **DNA paternity testing** makes bloodline verifiable. This article explains how the test works, what the headline probability really means, and how to use it without over-reading it.

## The Science

Paternity testing is based on **Mendelian inheritance** — offspring inherit half their genes from each parent. Testing compares alleles at multiple highly polymorphic **SNP loci**:

1. **Exclusion**: if a suspected parent lacks an allele the offspring must inherit, parentage is excluded.
2. **Confirmation**: if all alleles match, a **Paternity Index (PI)** and **Combined Probability** are computed to confirm.

[Sanshi Bio](/en/about/) uses **58 exclusive SNP loci** for accuracy.

## The Math Behind "99.99%"

The headline figure on a paternity report is not a direct measurement — it is a **computed probability**. Understanding the two-step calculation stops you from over-reading it.

First, each locus produces a **Paternity Index (PI)**: the ratio of "how likely is this genotype if the suspected bird is the true parent" versus "how likely if a random, unrelated bird is the parent." A PI above 1 favors parentage; a PI of 100 means the evidence is 100× more likely under the parent hypothesis.

Second, the individual PIs are multiplied across all loci into a **Combined Paternity Index (CPI)**, which is then converted into a probability using a neutral 50/50 prior:

**Probability of parentage = CPI ÷ (CPI + 1)**

This formula yields a clean ladder of thresholds:

| Combined Paternity Index (CPI) | Probability of parentage |
|---------------------------------|--------------------------|
| 9 | 90% |
| 99 | 99% |
| 999 | 99.9% |
| 9,999 | **99.99%** |
| 999,999 | 99.9999% |

So a report that prints "99.99%" is really saying the DNA evidence is roughly **10,000 times more likely** under the parent hypothesis than under a random-bird hypothesis. That is a strong statement — but it is a population-level calculation, which is exactly why no reputable lab ever prints "100%."

## What "99.99%" Does Not Mean

This is where fanciers' reading of reports tends to go wrong:

1. **It is not a claim of 100% certainty.** The number is capped below 100% by construction, because it compares against population allele frequencies rather than testing every bird alive.
2. **Single-parent testing is weaker.** Confirming against both parents gives far more power than testing against one parent alone. A motherless or fatherless comparison leaves more room for a non-excluded close relative to slip through.
3. **Close relatives confound the math.** The standard formula assumes the alternative is a random, unrelated bird. If the true parent could be the suspected bird's *sibling* — a real risk in lofts that keep full brothers — the standard CPI overstates confidence, and the lab should run a competing-candidate calculation instead.
4. **Locus count is not the whole story.** What matters is how *informative* each locus is. A biallelic SNP carries far less discrimination per locus than a multi-allelic forensic STR, which is why a 58-SNP panel behaves differently from the 20-STR panels used in human paternity work. The panel's real exclusion power depends on the minor-allele frequency of the loci chosen — the reason a well-selected 58-SNP set still excludes non-parents with over 99.9% confidence.

The honest summary: the test is excellent at **excluding** a wrong parent, and strong — but not absolute — at **confirming** a right one. Use it to catch forged pedigrees, and treat the printed percentage as strong evidence, not a DNA fingerprint of certainty.

## The Testing Process

1. **Consult and order** — determine the individuals and items to be tested
2. **Feather sampling** — pluck 4–6 breast feathers from offspring and suspected parents separately
3. **Laboratory analysis** — DNA extraction and 58-locus comparison
4. **Report issuance** — a conclusion including the parentage probability

## Deep Dive: SNP Loci and the Paternity Index

The core of paternity testing is comparing **polymorphic genetic markers**. Two marker types are commonly used:

| Marker | Feature | Use case |
|--------|---------|----------|
| STR (short tandem repeat) | High polymorphism, traditional in forensics | Human kinship testing |
| SNP (single nucleotide polymorphism) | Numerous, stable, easy to standardize | High-throughput individual identification |

Sanshi Bio uses **58 SNP loci**, whose advantages are the large locus count, stable genotyping and suitability for automated high-throughput testing.

The **Paternity Index (PI)** compares the probability of observing the current genotype under two hypotheses — "the suspected parent is the true parent" versus "a random individual is the parent." A larger PI means stronger evidence of kinship. Multiplying the PIs across loci yields the **Combined Paternity Index (CPI)**, converted into a **combined parentage probability** — 99.99% or higher confirms the relationship.

## A Practical Reading Checklist

When a paternity report lands, work through it in this order and you will not over-read or under-read the result:

1. **Confirm the sample IDs** match the birds you actually tested — a perfect result on the wrong bird is worthless.
2. **Check which comparison was run** — single-parent or dual-parent — because it sets the ceiling on confidence.
3. **Read the combined parentage probability** and, ideally, the Combined Paternity Index behind it — remember 9,999 ≈ 99.99%.
4. **Note any caveats** — close relatives in the flock, or loci that were non-informative — which the report should disclose.
5. **Use the result for its real purpose** — excluding a wrong parent or supporting a pedigree — rather than treating it as absolute proof.

The discipline in five steps is: verify, understand the comparison, read the number in context, note the limits, then act. Most mis-readings of paternity reports come from skipping step two and treating step three as step one.

## FAQ

### Do I need both parents for testing?
Both parents give a higher confirmation probability; single-parent testing is also possible but produces a weaker CPI and a lower ceiling on confidence. See [paternity testing](/en/paternity/).

### How accurate is paternity testing?
SNP-based parentage analysis can exclude non-parents with over 99.9% accuracy, and a combined parentage probability of 99.99% corresponds to a Combined Paternity Index of roughly 10,000 under the standard 50/50 prior.

### Why does no report ever say 100%?
Because the probability is a population statistic — it compares the evidence against the chance a random bird could match by coincidence. The formula (CPI ÷ (CPI + 1)) mathematically approaches but never reaches 100%.

### Can a sibling be mistaken for the parent?
It is possible with a single-parent comparison. The standard CPI assumes the alternative is an unrelated random bird, so if a full brother is a candidate, the lab should run a direct competing-candidate analysis instead.

### What is the difference between paternity testing and a gene ID card?
A [gene ID card](/en/blog/gene-id-card-guide/) is a unique identity profile for a single pigeon; paternity testing confirms the parent-offspring relationship between birds. Together they enable full pedigree tracing.

### Can squabs be paternity tested?
Yes. Feathers yield enough DNA, so young pigeons can be tested early for timely bloodline confirmation.

### What markers are used — STR or SNP?
Sanshi Bio uses 58 SNP loci, which are numerous, stable and ideal for automated high-throughput testing, rather than STR markers common in human forensics.

### What is the Paternity Index (PI)?
The PI compares the probability of the observed genotype under "suspected parent is the true parent" versus "a random individual is the parent." Larger PI means stronger evidence; the combined PI across loci converts to a parentage probability of 99.99% or higher for confirmation.
