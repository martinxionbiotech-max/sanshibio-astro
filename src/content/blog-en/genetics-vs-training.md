**Short answer:** Genes define a range of possibilities, not a finished result. A pigeon with favourable markers can still perform poorly if training, health or nutrition is wrong; a pigeon with ordinary markers can perform well under stable management.

## 1. Translate “potential” into something testable

“This bird has potential” usually means one of two things:

1. **Genotypic potential** — at certain markers, the bird carries versions that are associated with an advantage in a particular environment.
2. **Realised performance** — what the bird has actually done in training and racing.

Between them sit training, health, nutrition, recovery, stress and race conditions. Treating genotypic potential as realised performance is the most common overreach in pigeon selection.

## 2. The reasoning: same report, different outcomes

Imagine two birds with similar “favourable” flight-gene results.

### Case A — good genes, good management

- progressive training
- stable respiratory and digestive health
- nutrition and recovery matched to load
- sensible race schedule

The genetic range is more likely to be expressed.

### Case B — good genes, poor management

- too little or too much training
- untreated chronic infection
- nutritional gaps during moult
- frequent stress and inadequate recovery

The same genotype can produce a much lower performance.

### Case C — ordinary genes, stable management

- recorded training
- monitored health
- pairing and race schedule matched to the bird’s actual capacity

The bird may perform consistently and outperform a “better” genotype in a chaotic loft.

The point is not that genes do not matter. It is that:

```text
genotype × environment = phenotype
```

A multiplication sign means either side can drag the result toward zero.

## 3. What follows for selection

| Decision | Gene-only view | With management records |
|---|---|---|
| Retain a youngster | “This marker is good, keep it” | Also check health, development, training and pedigree |
| Pair two birds | “Pair the two good genotypes” | Also check conformation, race distance, offspring record |
| Cull a bird | “Ordinary genes, remove it” | First exclude management, health, sampling and report errors |
| Buy a bird | “The report looks great, buy it” | Also check race record, offspring, health and adaptation |

A more defensible order:

1. Confirm there is no obvious health or management deficit.
2. Read the gene report for direction.
3. Validate with race results, offspring and training records.
4. When genes and performance disagree, investigate the environment before dismissing the genes.

## 4. Editorial take

I do not like selling testing through genetic determinism, and I do not like dismissing testing through “training is everything.”

- **Testing is valuable** when information is scarce, especially for a youngster with no race record.
- **Management is valuable** because it determines whether the direction becomes a result.
- **Together** they turn selection from guesswork into a record with a hypothesis and a way to check it.

If a bird has a good report but a long record of poor results, I would first ask: is the training appropriate? Is health stable? Was moult nutrition adequate? Was the sample and report reliable? Until those questions are excluded, “bad genes” is not a sufficient explanation.

## 5. Boundaries

This is a general explanation of genetics and environment, not a training protocol or veterinary advice. Race distance, climate and loft management change the conclusion. A gene report provides probability and direction; it does not replace observation, health management or performance records.
