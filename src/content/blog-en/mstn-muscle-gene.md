# Racing Pigeon Muscle Gene MSTN

**Short answer:** MSTN encodes myostatin — a protein that *limits* muscle growth. Knock out or weaken it, and muscle mass rises; in pigeons, the studied marker is a **silent C→T mutation (g.11440232C>T)** where the T allele is **more common in higher-muscle-mass birds**. It is the "power" gene for sprint racing, but the price of extra muscle is extra weight.

> **TL;DR**: MSTN is the negative regulator of muscle growth — the "brake" on muscle development. Its discovery in 1997, and the natural "double-muscling" of Belgian Blue cattle, made it the textbook muscle gene. In pigeons the marker is a silent mutation, so its effect is likely indirect — but it is still the strongest available signal of sprint-power potential.

In short-distance racing, speed wins — and speed is built on muscle power. The development of a pigeon's flight (pectoral) muscles is regulated by the **MSTN (myostatin)** gene.

## What Is Myostatin?

MSTN encodes **myostatin**, a protein that **negatively regulates muscle growth** — it acts as a "brake" on muscle development.

| MSTN Activity | Muscle Development | Flight Profile |
|---------------|-------------------|----------------|
| Higher (strong inhibition) | Leaner muscle | Endurance-type |
| Lower (weak inhibition) | More developed muscle | Speed / burst-type |

## MSTN and Flight Speed

Lower MSTN activity means more developed pectoral muscles, giving:

- Stronger **burst power** for short-distance sprinting
- Higher **flight speed**
- Better fit for short-to-middle distance speed races

In the [flight ability gene test](/en/flight-ability/), MSTN corresponds to the **muscle power indicator** — the core measure of a pigeon's muscle strength and sprint-speed potential.

## The "Double-Muscling" Story, Explained

Myostatin is famous for a reason. When researchers discovered it in 1997, they did so by knocking it out in mice — the result was dramatically overgrown muscle. Nature had already run the same experiment: **Belgian Blue cattle** carry a natural MSTN mutation that produces the "double-muscling" phenotype, with visibly hypertrophied muscle and reduced fat. In chickens, CRISPR-mediated MSTN knockout produces significantly larger breast and leg muscles, and MSTN-deficient animals show a shift toward fast-twitch **Type 2B fibers** at the expense of oxidative **Type 1 and 2A fibers**.

This fiber-type shift is the useful analogy for pigeons: less myostatin activity tilts muscle toward **fast-twitch, glycolytic** fibers — the raw material of explosive sprint speed — but away from the oxidative fibers that sustain long flight. That is the trade-off in a nutshell.

## What the Pigeon Data Shows — And Its Wrinkle

The studied pigeon marker is **g.11440232C>T**, a C→T change in exon 3 at codon 287. Dybus et al. (2013, 2018) reported the **T allele was more common in higher-muscle-mass pigeons**.

But here is the wrinkle every fancier should understand: **this is a silent mutation**. The codon changes from ACC to ACT, yet **both encode threonine** — the myostatin protein's amino-acid sequence is unchanged. A silent mutation cannot, by itself, alter the protein. So the T allele's association with muscle mass is almost certainly a **linked-marker effect**: the SNP sits near, and is inherited together with, the actual causal variant elsewhere in the region.

The practical consequence: the MSTN marker is a *flag*, not a *mechanism*. It can still be a useful selection signal within a population, but its strength depends on how tightly it is linked to the causal variant in *your* gene pool — another reason the marker must be validated against your own birds' records, not assumed to work identically everywhere.

## Speed vs. Endurance

There is a trade-off between muscle mass (speed) and sustained flight (endurance). Over-developed muscle can add weight and hurt long-distance endurance:

- **Short/middle distance**: prioritize MSTN (muscle power)
- **Long distance**: prioritize [LDHA (endurance)](/en/blog/ldha-endurance-gene/) and [CRY1 (navigation)](/en/blog/cry1-navigation-gene/)

The ideal pigeon finds a genetic balance between muscle and endurance matched to its race program.

## Breeding Recommendations

- Fanciers racing speed events can prioritize favorable MSTN genotypes.
- Combine with the [DRD4 (homing)](/en/blog/drd4-homing-gene/) indicator to balance speed with stability.
- Squabs can be tested early to lock in speed potential from the start.

## Reading Your Report: Muscle Power in Context

On the [flight ability gene test](/en/flight-ability/) report, the muscle-power indicator is one of eight — and it should never be read in isolation. A strong MSTN result tells you the bird has sprint potential; it says nothing about whether it will come home. Pair it with [DRD4](/en/blog/drd4-homing-gene/) for homing persistence and [F-KER](/en/blog/f-ker-feather-gene/) for wing efficiency, and only then do you have a picture of a complete sprinter.

The same logic applies in reverse: a weak muscle-power result does not make a bird useless. A lean, endurance-leaning pigeon may be exactly what a 700 km+ program needs. The report's value is not in ranking birds "good" or "bad" on one gene — it is in matching each bird's genotype to the distance it is actually going to race.

## Technical Deep Dive: The Myostatin Signaling Pathway

Myostatin is a member of the **TGF-β superfamily** (also called growth/differentiation factor 8, GDF8). It binds activin receptors and, through Smad signaling, inhibits muscle precursor proliferation and differentiation — the molecular "brake" on muscle mass. When MSTN is mutated or down-regulated, the brake releases and muscle mass increases, the "double-muscling" phenotype seen across mammals, birds and fish.

In racing pigeons, MSTN genotype differences shape the development and burst power of the pectoral muscles. Individuals with lower MSTN activity develop fuller pectoral muscle and stronger short-distance sprinting; but over-developed muscle can also add body weight and hurt long-distance endurance, so a balance between speed and endurance must be struck.

## Breeding in Practice: Selecting for Speed

- **Match the event**: prioritize favorable MSTN genotypes for short-to-middle distance speed races where burst power is decisive.
- **Keep balance in mind**: do not over-pursue muscle for long-distance races — combine with [endurance LDHA](/en/blog/ldha-endurance-gene/) in a holistic assessment.
- **Train for power**: pair speed-type pigeons with burst training (short sprints, climb training) to amplify their muscle advantage.
- **Manage body weight**: control weight to prevent over-developed muscle from hurting flight efficiency.

## FAQ

### Does more muscle always mean faster flight?
Not always. Developed muscle boosts burst speed, but excessive mass may hurt endurance. Balance muscle with endurance by race profile.

### How do breeding priorities differ between speed and endurance races?
Short-to-middle distance speed races prioritize the muscle indicator (MSTN); long-distance endurance races prioritize endurance (LDHA) and navigation ([CRY1](/en/blog/cry1-navigation-gene/)).

### Is MSTN genotype linked to muscle mass?
Yes. Some genotypes associate with higher muscle mass — the T allele of g.11440232C>T was more common in heavier-muscled pigeons. But note this SNP is silent, so the effect is likely indirect (a linked marker), not a change to myostatin itself.

### What is "double muscling" and does it apply to pigeons?
Double muscling is the hypertrophied-muscle phenotype seen when myostatin is disabled, best known in Belgian Blue cattle and MSTN-knockout mice and chickens. Pigeons don't carry that extreme mutation, but the same pathway shapes their muscle mass.

### Can MSTN and LDHA both be optimized?
They are independent loci and can be tested together. Speed and endurance involve some trade-off — see the [speed vs endurance guide](/en/blog/speed-distance-gene/).

### Does MSTN testing require blood sampling?
No. Plucking 4–6 feathers with follicles is enough to extract DNA, and squabs can be tested.

### Does a heavily muscled pigeon always fly fast?
Not necessarily. Muscle mass provides the burst-power foundation, but flight speed is also affected by wing structure, body condition and husbandry — evaluate holistically.

## Key Takeaways

1. MSTN is the power marker — myostatin negatively regulates muscle growth.
2. Genotype drives explosiveness — weaker myostatin means more muscle and power.
3. The pigeon marker is a silent SNP — its effect is likely a linked marker, not a direct protein change.
4. Prioritize for sprint races — 200–400 km races favor strong MSTN genotypes.
5. Distinct from endurance — MSTN powers sprints, LDHA sustains distance.
