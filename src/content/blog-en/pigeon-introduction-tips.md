> **TL;DR**: Introduction is key to improving a loft, but it is also the highest-risk moment for disease entry. The quarantine numbers that matter: at least **14 days** as an absolute floor, **30 days** as the widely recommended standard, and up to **six weeks** to fully evaluate a bird's disease status — because a bird can look healthy and still carry disease. Scientific introduction should follow "bloodline first, then health, then quarantine."

Bringing in one great breeder can launch a loft to new heights; bringing in one sick bird can drag the whole flock down. There are many "racing pigeon introduction tips," but they boil down to one core rule: **don't let a new bird become an entry point for disease**. This article walks through the complete flow from selection to quarantine, helping you minimize introduction risk.

## Before You Introduce: Know What You Want

Introduction is not "buy on sight." Clarify your goals before you act:

| Decision | Questions to ask |
|--------|--------------|
| Purpose | Add speed? Add endurance? Improve bloodline? |
| Race positioning | Sprint or long-distance endurance |
| Budget & quantity | One elite bird, or a whole group |
| Bloodline verification | Can you verify the parents and pedigree authenticity |

Once your goals are clear, you can enter selection without "paying for a lesson."

## The Core Risk: Healthy-Looking Birds Can Still Be Carriers

The single most important fact about introduction is that **appearance is not evidence of health**. A bird can carry Salmonella, circovirus or mycoplasma while looking bright, eating well and flying normally — disease takes time to show, and some pathogens never show at all in a carrier. This is why every quarantine protocol exists: it buys *time* for latent infection to surface or be detected, rather than trusting a first-day inspection.

Veterinary guidance puts concrete numbers on that time. The practical floor is **14 days** of isolation; the widely recommended standard is **30 days**, with the disease status of a new bird sometimes taking up to **six weeks** to fully evaluate. Birds arriving from multiple different sources warrant even longer — up to 90 days in stricter protocols. The point is not the exact number but the principle: quarantine until *testing plus observation* both clear the bird, not until you feel impatient.

## Selection Checklist

| Point | Explanation |
|------|------|
| Check the pedigree | Request a complete pedigree and verify parent ring numbers and race records |
| Check health | Feather sheen, energy, droppings and breathing |
| Check age | Squabs, young birds and adults suit different purposes |
| Check the source environment | Original loft hygiene, density, and disease history |
| Agree on quarantine | Settle post-introduction health screening and responsibility upfront |

> ⚠️ Note: Pedigrees can be forged or messy, and health status can be masked by "temporary treatment." What is truly reliable is **quarantine observation + objective testing** after introduction.

## The Complete Quarantine Flow

For new birds entering the loft, follow this quarantine flow strictly:

1. **Separate isolation** — house new birds separately, physically isolated from the existing flock (ideally a separate loft or cage)
2. **Observation** — watch feed intake, drinking, droppings and mental state daily during quarantine
3. **Pathogen testing** — sample during quarantine for [pathogen testing](/en/virus-detection/) to screen for latent infection
4. **Bloodline verification** — confirm parentage and pedigree authenticity via [paternity testing](/en/paternity/)
5. **Integrate after clearance** — gradually mix with the flock after testing passes and observation is normal

The length of quarantine varies by individual and source risk; consult a veterinarian to confirm, usually no less than several weeks.

Two discipline points make or break the flow. First, **all-in, all-out**: if any new bird is added to the quarantine area mid-period, the clock restarts for everyone. Second, **handle the quarantine birds last** — feed, clean and manage your main flock first, then the new arrivals, so you never carry contamination back on your hands or clothing.

## What Diseases to Screen For

Quarantine should focus on pathogens that transmit vertically through breeders or horizontally within the loft:

| Pathogen type | Representative pathogen | Why it matters |
|----------|----------|----------|
| Viral | Newcastle disease, avian influenza, circovirus | Highly contagious; some are immunosuppressive |
| Bacterial | Salmonella (paratyphoid) | Vertically transmitted, harms breeders |
| Mycoplasma | Pigeon mycoplasma | Chronic respiratory disease, recurring |
| Parasitic | Trichomonas | Widespread, needs routine control |

## Bloodline Verification: The Role of Paternity Testing

A pedigree is a "paper promise"; paternity testing is "scientific verification." Running [paternity testing](/en/paternity/) at introduction lets you:

- Confirm the true parent-offspring relationship between a squab and its labeled parents
- Verify pedigree authenticity and avoid "nominal bloodlines"
- Provide an objective basis for high-value purchases and lower transaction risk

> 💡 Tip: Making "introduction + pathogen testing + paternity testing" a standard routine is common practice among mature lofts.

## The Documentation Habit That Protects the Purchase

Introduction risk is reduced most by a habit that costs nothing: **paper every bird that enters the loft**. For each new arrival, keep a single record that ties together the documents you should already be collecting:

- The **pedigree** as claimed, with parent ring numbers and race records;
- The **quarantine log** — dates, observations, and any abnormalities;
- The **test results** — pathogen screening and, for high-value birds, the paternity report;
- The **integration date** — when the bird actually joined the flock, and why.

This record does two jobs. First, it forces the discipline that makes quarantine real: if the log is empty, the quarantine was not done. Second, it protects you later — if a bird sickens, you have a timeline; if a pedigree is disputed, you have the verification you ran. A loft that keeps these records turns introduction from a leap of faith into a reviewed process, and that is the difference between a loft that occasionally has disease trouble and one that rarely does.

## FAQ

### How long should a new pigeon be quarantined?
At least 14 days as a floor, with 30 days the widely recommended standard — and the disease status of a new bird can take up to six weeks to fully evaluate. Complete pathogen testing and confirm no abnormalities before mixing with the flock.

### Can a healthy-looking bird still carry disease?
Yes. Salmonella, circovirus and mycoplasma can be carried asymptomatically — bright feathers and normal appetite are not proof of health. That is exactly why quarantine observation and objective testing matter.

### Is pathogen testing mandatory for introduction?
Strongly recommended. New birds may carry latent pathogens (such as Salmonella or circovirus) — a healthy appearance does not mean disease-free. Pathogen testing is the core of quarantine.

### Can a pedigree be fully trusted?
No. Pedigrees can be forged or have messy records; verify parentage objectively through paternity testing.

### What should I watch during quarantine?
Focus on feed and water intake, droppings, mental state and breathing. Any abnormality should be isolated and tested to avoid spread.

### Do I need paternity testing for introduction?
If the purchase is expensive or the bird is for breeding, yes. Paternity testing confirms the true relationship between a squab and its labeled parents, providing an objective basis for the transaction.

### Can I mix the flock right after quarantine passes?
It is best to meet both "testing passed" and "no abnormalities in observation" before gradually integrating, to avoid gaps from insufficient observation.

### Why should I handle quarantine birds last?
To avoid carrying contamination back to the main flock. Feed, clean and manage your existing birds first, then the new arrivals — a simple order-of-operations rule that prevents accidental spread.

## Key Takeaways

1. Clarify purpose and budget before introduction to avoid blind purchases.
2. Verify the pedigree — don't just read it — to guard against forgery.
3. New birds must be isolated separately to keep disease out of the loft (14 days floor, 30 days standard).
4. Run pathogen testing during quarantine to screen for latent infection — a healthy look is not proof.
5. Paternity testing objectively verifies bloodline, backing up high-value purchases.
