## Self-Built Lab vs. Send-Out Testing: The Breakeven Math

Many fanciers hesitate between "buying equipment to build their own lab" and "sending samples out." The cost logic is completely different:

| Dimension | Self-built lab | Send-out testing |
|-----------|----------------|------------------|
| Upfront investment | One-time equipment purchase (price requires consultation) | No equipment investment |
| Per-test cost | High upfront, low marginal cost later | Pay per item |
| Flexibility | Test anytime, fast results | Depends on logistics and lab scheduling |
| Suitable for | Professional lofts, one-loft races, testing agencies | Casual fanciers, low-frequency testing |

The decision reduces to a simple breakeven sum. A self-built lab has a **fixed upfront cost** (equipment + training) plus a small **variable reagent cost per test**; send-out testing has **no fixed cost** but a higher per-bird fee. The self-built lab wins once your annual volume crosses the point where:

**Fixed cost ÷ (send-out fee − per-test reagent cost) = breakeven number of tests**

Worked example with round numbers: if send-out costs roughly tens of dollars per bird and a self-built lab's per-test reagent cost is a small fraction of that, then a fixed equipment outlay of a few thousand dollars pays for itself after a few hundred tests. Test far below that volume and you are better off mailing samples; test far above it and every additional bird gets cheaper. The key is to estimate your *real* annual volume honestly — the equipment that "pays for itself" only pays if you actually run the tests.

## Hidden Costs That Catch Lofts Out

The sticker price of the instrument is rarely the whole cost of a self-built lab. Four recurring items quietly decide whether the project is cheaper than mail-in testing, and they are easy to overlook when comparing quotes:

1. **Reagents and consumables.** Every run consumes extraction kits, master mix, plates and tips. These are the "small" costs that dominate long-run spending — an instrument is bought once, but reagents are bought forever.
2. **Training and technique.** An untrained operator produces unreliable results, and reliability is the entire point. Budget for initial training and occasional re-training as staff or methods change.
3. **Calibration and maintenance.** Pipettes need annual calibration; cyclers and extractors need scheduled verification. Skipped maintenance degrades results silently.
4. **The cost of an idle machine.** An instrument that runs twice a year still ages, still needs reagents kept in date, and still ties up capital. This is the single most common way a "cost-saving" lab loses money.

Add these up honestly before you buy, and the breakeven test becomes clear: the self-built lab wins only where genuine, ongoing volume justifies it. For most lofts, the disciplined answer is mail-in testing plus a modest setup — and treating the equipment purchase as a luxury, not a default.
