> **Short answer**: Newcastle disease in racing pigeons — technically pigeon paramyxovirus type 1 (PPMV-1) — kills on average roughly **40% of infected birds**, with experimental and severe outbreaks reported as high as **80–100%**. The incubation window runs **2–15 days**, and infected birds shed virus *before* symptoms show, which is why pre-race PCR screening, not symptom-watching, is the only control that actually works.

**Newcastle disease (ND)** is one of the most serious viral infections in racing pigeons. It is caused by a paramyxovirus — specifically **pigeon paramyxovirus type 1 (PPMV-1)**, a host-adapted variant of avian paramyxovirus 1 (APMV-1) — with high contagiousness and mortality. In naive flocks, morbidity and mortality can approach **100%**, but the more realistic field picture for racing lofts is a commonly cited **~40% mortality**, with experimental infections and poorly managed outbreaks reaching **80–90%** ([MSD Veterinary Manual](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/poultry/newcastle-disease-and-other-avian-paramyxovirus-infections/newcastle-disease-in-poultry)). That spread — from a survivable scare to a near-total wipeout — is precisely why the numbers matter to a fancier: a 40% loss is a bad season; a 90% loss is a loft-ending catastrophe.

## Pathogen & Transmission

Newcastle disease virus (NDV/PPMV-1) mainly spreads through:

| Route | Description |
|-------|-------------|
| Respiratory | Aerosols from coughing and sneezing |
| Digestive | Contaminated water and feed |
| Contact | Direct contact with sick birds |
| Mechanical | Carried by people, equipment and vehicles |

The virus is a **single-stranded, negative-sense RNA** paramyxovirus in the family *Paramyxoviridae*. Two surface glycoproteins do the damage: the **F (fusion) protein**, which fuses the viral and host membranes and is the primary determinant of virulence, and the **HN (hemagglutinin-neuraminidase) protein**, which handles attachment and release. This is not academic detail — the F protein cleavage site is how laboratories and regulators grade whether a strain is virulent ("velogenic"), moderately virulent ("mesogenic") or mild ("lentogenic"), and it is why one outbreak can look like a mild respiratory blip while another kills half a loft in a week.

## Clinical Signs

PPMV-1 in pigeons classically presents in three overlapping clusters:

- **Respiratory**: rapid breathing, open-mouth breathing, coughing, nasal discharge
- **Digestive**: green watery diarrhea, loss of appetite
- **Neurological** (the tell-tale sign): head tilt (torticollis), circling, wing droop, leg paralysis, head tremors
- **Reproductive**: sudden drop in egg production, thin-shelled eggs

The neurological signs are the most characteristic feature and the one that separates ND from an ordinary "one-eye cold" or respiratory complaint. A pigeon walking in circles or holding its head twisted at an odd angle, in a loft with recent losses, should be treated as ND until proven otherwise.

## The Numbers That Drive Control

Three figures shape every control decision:

1. **Incubation: 2–15 days.** Clinical signs can appear as early as 2 days and as late as 15 days after infection ([MSD Veterinary Manual](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/poultry/newcastle-disease-and-other-avian-paramyxovirus-infections/newcastle-disease-in-poultry)). Critically, birds shed virus *during* this window — a pigeon can be infectious and look perfectly normal.
2. **Mortality: ~40% typical, up to 80–100%.** A UK government bulletin states PPMV mortality can vary between **10% and 100%** ([States of Guernsey](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=136165&p=0)); the pigeon variant can produce **morbidity up to 80%** ([CFIA](https://inspection.canada.ca/en/animal-health/terrestrial-animals/diseases/reportable/nd/hazard-specific-plan/newcastle-disease-overview)).
3. **Shedding precedes signs.** This is the false-negative trap: waiting for symptoms means you detect the outbreak *after* it has already seeded the loft.

Do the arithmetic on a 50-bird racing team: a 40% mortality rate means 20 birds dead; at the experimental upper bound of 90%, only 5 survive. The difference between those two outcomes is almost entirely down to how early the outbreak is found and isolated.

## Why Early Detection Matters

Newcastle disease has a short incubation period and spreads fast — by the time obvious signs appear, large-scale infection has usually occurred. **Early screening** is therefore the core of control.

[Sanshi Bio virus detection](/en/virus-detection/) uses **real-time PCR** to detect viral nucleic acid before obvious symptoms appear, enabling early isolation. This matters because PCR turns the 2–15 day incubation window into usable lead time: a pre-race swab that finds PPMV-1 in an apparently healthy bird lets you pull that bird *before* it enters a shared basket and infects the rest of the team.

## Scientific Control Strategy

1. **Vaccination** — follow the standard immunization program, and confirm it worked with [antibody titer testing](/en/blog/antibody-titer-test/). Protection is not 100%, and strain mismatch is real — a vaccine against the wrong PPMV-1 genotype blunts disease but does not stop shedding.
2. **Pre-race screening** — test before racing and introducing new birds. This closes the "carrier enters the basket" route.
3. **Isolation management** — isolate and test newly introduced pigeons for 2–4 weeks.
4. **Environmental disinfection** — regularly disinfect the loft and equipment.
5. **Biosecurity** — reduce entry of outside personnel and vehicles.

## What to Do When Symptoms Appear

1. Immediately isolate suspected sick birds
2. Collect oral and cloacal swabs for testing
3. Consult a veterinarian — do not medicate blindly; there is no specific antiviral for ND
4. Thoroughly disinfect the loft

> ⚠️ Note: Newcastle disease is a highly contagious disease and in many jurisdictions a notifiable one. Isolate and test at the first suspicion to avoid spread, and report per local veterinary authority requirements.

## Immunization & Biosecurity in Practice

Vaccination is the backbone, but it is not a guarantee. The WOAH considers an HI (haemagglutination-inhibition) antibody titre of **log₂ 4 (1:16)** or higher against 4 HAU of antigen to be the positive threshold ([WOAH Terrestrial Manual](https://www.woah.org/fileadmin/Home/fr/Health_standards/tahm/3.03.14_NEWCASTLE_DIS.pdf)); field studies treat titres below **log₂ 4** as non-protective, and herd immunity only holds when **more than 85% of birds** carry a protective titre ([van Boven et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2556191/)). That last number is the quiet killer of lofts that "vaccinate but still lose birds": if a quarter of your team never seroconverted, the flock has a hole in it no calendar schedule will fix.

1. **Standard immunization** — vaccinate per your veterinarian's schedule, matching the vaccine strain to circulating strains.
2. **Pre-race screening** — screen for the [pathogen](/en/virus-detection/) before racing or basketing, preventing carriers from entering.
3. **Quarantine new birds** — isolate newly introduced pigeons for 2–4 weeks and test for pathogens.
4. **Environmental disinfection** — regularly disinfect the loft, feeders and drinkers.
5. **Reduce stress** — transport and basketing stress can trigger latent infection; manage stress carefully.

## The Author's Take

**Position**: In my view, the single highest-value investment a racing loft can make against Newcastle disease is pre-race PCR screening — not a better vaccine, and certainly not a broader medicine cabinet.

**Reasoning**: First, the virus is shed during a 2–15 day incubation window before any sign appears, so symptom-based management always arrives late. Second, vaccination alone cannot be trusted to reach the >85% herd-immunity threshold in every flock — titre testing shows individual non-responders every season. Third, a single carrier entering a shared race basket converts your loft's internal control problem into an external one you can no longer control. Pre-race screening closes the one transmission route that hygiene and vaccination cannot.

**Disclosure**: This is my editorial judgment based on the published transmission dynamics above, not a claim from a controlled field trial, and it does not replace veterinary guidance on vaccination schedules.

## FAQ

### Can Newcastle disease be cured?
No specific cure exists — it is a viral disease, so prevention (vaccination + biosecurity) is key. Once it appears, isolate promptly, prevent spread and provide veterinary supportive care. Early [detection](/en/virus-detection/) minimizes loss.

### What is the incubation period?
Typically **2–15 days** depending on strain virulence and individual immunity ([MSD Veterinary Manual](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/poultry/newcastle-disease-and-other-avian-paramyxovirus-infections/newcastle-disease-in-poultry)). Infected birds can already shed virus during incubation, making pre-race screening especially important.

### How deadly is Newcastle disease in pigeons?
Field mortality is commonly around **40%**, but experimental infections and severe outbreaks report **80–90%** ([MSD Veterinary Manual](https://www.msdvetmanual.com/poultry/newcastle-disease-and-other-avian-paramyxovirus-infections/newcastle-disease-in-poultry)); a UK government bulletin cites a **10–100%** range depending on the flock and strain ([States of Guernsey](https://www.gov.gg/CHttpHandler.ashx?id=136165&p=0)).

### How does Newcastle disease differ from the common cold?
Early signs overlap (respiratory), but Newcastle disease is often accompanied by green watery diarrhea and neurological signs (head tilt, circling), spreads fast and has high mortality. Confirm by [real-time PCR](/en/virus-detection/) rather than treating on experience.

### How does Newcastle disease differ from avian influenza?
Different virus families (paramyxovirus vs orthomyxovirus) with overlapping symptoms but different control. Newcastle disease is more common and more lethal in racing pigeons. Testing distinguishes them — see [avian influenza screening](/en/blog/avian-influenza-screening/).

### Can vaccinated pigeons still be infected?
Yes — protection is not 100%, and strain mismatch occurs: an incorrectly matched PPMV-1 genotype can blunt clinical disease while the bird still sheds virus. Check [antibody titer testing](/en/blog/antibody-titer-test/) to evaluate immunity and boost when titers drop below the protective threshold.

### What does a protective antibody titre look like?
The WOAH treats an HI titre of **log₂ 4 (1:16)** or higher as positive ([WOAH Terrestrial Manual](https://www.woah.org/fileadmin/Home/fr/Health_standards/tahm/3.03.14_NEWCASTLE_DIS.pdf)). Flock-level protection requires a high proportion of birds — field modelling puts the herd-immunity threshold above **85%** with a protective titre ([van Boven et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2556191/)).

### What should I do if a bird tests positive?
Immediately isolate positive birds, thoroughly disinfect the loft, and consult a veterinarian on disposal per local regulations. Newcastle disease is highly contagious — do not delay.

## Key Takeaways

1. Caused by a paramyxovirus (PPMV-1) — highly contagious and highly lethal: ~40% typical mortality, up to 80–100% in severe cases.
2. Incubation runs 2–15 days, with virus shed *before* symptoms appear — the false-negative window.
3. Three symptom clusters — respiratory, digestive and neurological (head tilt is the tell).
4. Early detection is critical — qPCR detects the pathogen before symptoms appear.
5. Vaccination is necessary but not sufficient — protection is not 100%, and herd immunity needs >85% of birds at protective titre.
6. No specific cure — prevention first; isolate early to limit losses.
