**Short answer:** A laboratory is not a shopping list. It is a controlled sample flow. Decide the tests, sample volume and reporting model first; then choose equipment. Otherwise you can buy instruments and still fail to produce repeatable, defensible results.

## 1. Draw the workflow before buying anything

A testing lab has at least three stages:

```text
pre-analytical: receipt, ID, storage, extraction
analytical: qPCR or other assay, controls, interpretation
post-analytical: report, review, retention, waste
```

If any stage is uncontrolled, a more expensive instrument will not rescue the result. The first task is a flow diagram: who does what, where, and how it is recorded.

## 2. The reasoning: three practical tiers

### Entry tier — sampling and send-out

For a small loft or service point with low volume.

- standard sampling, ID, storage and shipping
- sample register and report archive
- partnership with an external laboratory
- no claim to perform every test in-house

Low investment and low risk; turnaround and interpretation depend on the partner lab.

### Intermediate tier — extraction and qPCR in-house

For a stable sample volume and a need for faster turnaround.

- separate sample-processing area
- nucleic-acid extraction
- qPCR with negative, positive and internal controls
- contamination control and zoning
- report review and sample retention

The critical issue is not the qPCR machine. It is stable extraction, controls, contamination prevention and someone competent to interpret abnormal results.

### Professional tier — multi-assay, multi-batch, auditable

For a service laboratory with multiple projects.

- unidirectional sample flow and stricter zoning
- equipment calibration and maintenance records
- reagent lot and expiry control
- training and access permissions
- quality system, complaints and re-test process
- data backup and report version control

This is not “buy the most expensive instruments.” It is “make every step auditable.”

## 3. What follows: questions before building

| Question | Why it matters |
|---|---|
| Which tests will you run? | Determines equipment, reagents and zoning |
| Monthly sample volume? | Determines throughput and staffing |
| Who collects samples? | Determines sample-quality responsibility |
| Who signs out reports? | Determines review capability |
| What happens with an abnormal result? | Determines re-test and complaint flow |
| How is contamination controlled? | Determines layout and controls |
| How long is data kept? | Determines systems and privacy |
| Will you serve external clients? | Determines quality-system and legal exposure |

## 4. Editorial take

I would not start with “full in-house” by default.

- If volume is too low, the quality-control cost can exceed send-out testing.
- If sample flow and review are unstable, qPCR only scales the errors.
- If you serve external clients, design the quality system, report responsibility and re-test path before upgrading equipment.

The most economical sequence is usually:

```text
sampling and sample chain
→ extraction and qPCR
→ more assays and a quality system
```

## 5. Boundaries

This is a planning framework, not an equipment quotation or budget. Requirements differ by assay, throughput, site and staff. Biosafety, waste and clinical reporting must follow local law and professional standards.
