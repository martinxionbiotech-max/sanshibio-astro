> **Short answer**: Weight loss in pigeons is a shared expression of many diseases, not a single illness. The usual suspects are parasites (*Trichomonas gallinae*, coccidia, roundworm), bacteria (*Salmonella*), and immunosuppressive viruses (PiCV) — and the numbers explain why: *T. gallinae* infects **~34% of pigeons (up to ~70% of squabs)**, and a virulent strain can kill **up to 90% of squabs**. Finding the cause requires **pathogen testing** — blindly "topping up nutrition" treats the symptom, not the cause.

"Why is my pigeon losing weight?" Many fanciers notice a bird that "eats plenty but never gains," growing thinner until "the keel feels like a knife blade," yet cannot find the reason. Weight loss (sometimes called "going light") is not a single disease but a **shared signal of many causes**. The phrase that should stick is "eats plenty but never gains" — because that specific pattern almost always points to a **parasite or pathogen consuming the nutrients**, not to a feeding deficiency.

## The Essence of Weight Loss

A pigeon's body weight comes from the balance between "intake" and "expenditure." Weight loss means that balance is broken:

- **Insufficient intake**: reduced eating, or poor digestion and absorption;
- **Excessive expenditure**: parasites robbing nutrients, pathogens draining energy, or chronic inflammation consuming it.

So identifying who is on the "expenditure side" is the key to solving the problem. The diagnostic shortcut is the bird's appetite: a bird that *eats well but still loses* points to a parasite or malabsorption problem; a bird that *eats less* may simply be ill or stressed. Appetite is the first branch of the decision tree.

## Common Cause Categories

| Cause type | Common representatives | Weight-loss features | Other clues |
|------------|------------------------|----------------------|-------------|
| Parasites | Trichomonas, coccidia, roundworm | Progressive loss, eats plenty but never gains | Green or pasty droppings, anemia |
| Bacterial | Salmonella (paratyphoid) | Loss with diarrhea | Swollen joints, neurological signs |
| Viral/immunosuppressive | Pigeon Circovirus, adenovirus | Poor growth and loss in young birds | Recurrent infections, abnormal feathers |
| Nutrition/management | Unbalanced feed, high density | Whole flock thin | No specific signs |

## Rule Out Parasites First — the Prime Suspect

**Trichomonas** inhabits the mouth, crop and digestive tract, robbing nutrients and interfering with swallowing; **coccidia** damage the intestinal lining, causing poor absorption; **roundworms** and other internal parasites literally "compete for the food." Such infections often show as "eats plenty but thin, ruffled feathers, green or pasty droppings." See [Trichomonas and Candida control](/en/blog/trichomonas-candida-control/).

The prevalence data is why parasites are the default assumption. *T. gallinae* was found in **34.31% of domestic pigeons in a Beijing survey, and in 49.08% of squabs** ([Beijing study](https://doi.org/10.3390/ani14131869)); other surveys report 60% or higher, with squabs the most affected. And the stakes are not trivial: a highly virulent strain can cause **~50% mortality in adults and up to 90% in squabs** ([Assam study](https://arccjournals.com/journal/indian-journal-of-animal-research/B-3805)). A "thin" bird is often a *parasitized* bird.

## Bacteria and Viruses: the Hidden "Consumers"

- **Salmonella**: chronic infection often causes stubborn diarrhea and weight loss, and even joint and neurological signs — see [Pigeon Salmonella (Paratyphoid)](/en/blog/salmonella-paratyphoid/). One documented outbreak reached a **21% flock mortality** ([Iran study](https://jzd.tabrizu.ac.ir/article_10587.html)).
- **Pigeon Circovirus**: damages the immune system, causing "immunosuppression" that leaves the bird prone to repeated infections, poor growth and weight loss — see [Pigeon Circovirus and Adenovirus](/en/blog/pigeon-circovirus-adenovirus/). The weight loss here is indirect: the bird is not being eaten by the virus, it is losing to the *secondary infections* the immunosuppression invites.

## A Scientific Approach: Test First, Find the Cause

Weight loss has many overlapping causes, and the correct order is "find the cause first, then act":

1. **Check management factors**: review feed formula, feeding amount, stocking density and deworming records.
2. **Sample and test**: collect a pharyngeal swab, cloacal swab or feces for [pathogen detection](/en/virus-detection/) to screen for Trichomonas, coccidia, Salmonella and Circovirus in one run. See the [sample collection guide](/en/blog/sample-collection-guide/) for method.
3. **Treat accordingly**: after confirmation, medicate under veterinary guidance — bacterial infections can use **sensitivity testing**, and parasites should be dewormed according to a veterinary plan.
4. **Supportive care**: strengthen nutrition, replenish electrolytes, reduce stress and isolate sick birds.

> 💡 Tip: do not rush to "fatten up" a thin pigeon — find the pathogen that is "consuming" it. [Pathogen detection](/en/virus-detection/) can catch them all in one run; see the [pathogen encyclopedia](https://pathogens.sanshibio.net/) for more.

## The Author's Take

**Position**: In my view, "fatten up a thin pigeon" is the single most common and most counterproductive instinct in pigeon-keeping — because a bird that eats plenty and still loses is not underfed, it is being consumed, and piling on extra grain only feeds the parasite or pathogen doing the consuming.

**Reasoning**: First, the "eats plenty but never gains" pattern is a textbook parasite/malabsorption signature, and the Trichomonas prevalence figures (34% overall, ~50% in squabs) make that the default hypothesis, not the exception. Second, treating the visible symptom (thinness) while leaving the invisible cause (parasite, Salmonella, or immunosuppression) means the bird keeps losing regardless of how much you feed it. Third, the fix is cheap and decisive: one multiplex swab screens the likely culprits in a single run. Find the consumer, then decide what to do about it.

**Disclosure**: This is my editorial judgment based on published prevalence and mortality data, not a claim that every thin pigeon has a parasite. Management causes are real and should be ruled out first.

## FAQ

### Why is my pigeon losing weight?
Weight loss is a shared expression of many causes — commonly parasites (Trichomonas, coccidia, roundworm), chronic or immunosuppressive diseases (Salmonella, Pigeon Circovirus), or unbalanced nutrition. Testing is needed to identify the cause.

### Why does my pigeon eat plenty but never gain weight?
This usually points to parasites (Trichomonas, roundworm, coccidia) robbing nutrients or intestinal absorption problems, and may also relate to immunosuppressive disease. Run [pathogen detection](/en/virus-detection/) to investigate.

### Should I deworm a thin pigeon?
Deworming should target the specific parasite — do not medicate blindly. Sample and test first to confirm whether parasites are present and which species, then deworm under veterinary guidance.

### Can a thin pigeon still race?
Weight loss itself shows the bird is unhealthy — forcing it to race will worsen the condition and the result will hardly be good. Find and fix the cause and restore condition before considering training and racing.

### Is pigeon weight loss contagious?
It depends on the cause. Parasites, Salmonella and Circovirus can all spread through the flock. Isolate thin birds promptly and test to prevent spread.

### How do I test a thin pigeon?
Collect a pharyngeal swab, cloacal swab or fresh feces to screen for Trichomonas, coccidia, Salmonella and Circovirus in one run. See the [sample collection guide](/en/blog/sample-collection-guide/).

## Key Takeaways

1. Weight loss is a shared signal of many causes, not a single disease.
2. "Eats plenty but never gains" points to parasites — the prime suspects.
3. Trichomonas infects ~34% of pigeons (~50% of squabs) and can kill up to 90% of squabs.
4. Chronic or immunosuppressive infections like Salmonella and Circovirus also cause loss.
5. Rule out management factors first, then pinpoint the cause with pathogen testing.
6. After confirmation, treat under veterinary guidance with nutritional support.
