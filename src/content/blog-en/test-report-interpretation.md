**Short answer:** Read the report in three passes. First confirm that the sample ID and ring number match the bird. Then read the flight-gene genotypes as potential — not a race prediction. Then read the pathogen or paternity section against its own reference range. A report is a decision aid, not a guarantee of performance or health.

A gene test report can look technical because it contains genotypes, Ct values and probability figures. The useful way to read it is not to memorise every letter; it is to separate four questions:

1. **Is this the right bird?**
2. **What was tested?**
3. **What does the result actually support?**
4. **What decision should follow — if any?**

## 1. The report structure

A complete report normally contains:

| Section | What it should tell you |
|---|---|
| Sample information | Bird ID, ring number, sample type, collection date |
| Test scope | Flight genes, pathogens, paternity or identity |
| Results | Genotype, Ct value or parentage probability |
| Interpretation | What the marker is associated with, and its limits |
| Recommendations | Breeding, re-test or veterinary follow-up — bounded by the evidence |

**First check the sample information.** If the ring number, sample ID or collection date is wrong, the result cannot be tied to the bird. This is the cheapest error to catch and the most expensive one to miss.

## 2. Flight-gene section

A flight-ability report typically covers eight markers:

| Trait | Gene | What the result can support |
|---|---|---|
| Endurance | LDHA | A genetic predisposition worth considering in distance-oriented selection |
| Homing | DRD4 | A behavioural/neurological association, not a guarantee of homing |
| Navigation | CRY1 | A magnetoreception-related association; environment still matters |
| Muscle | MSTN | A muscle-growth-related marker; not a direct speed score |
| Feather | F-KER | A feather-keratin marker; not a full plumage assessment |
| Memory | LRP8 | A cognitive/learning association; training still matters |
| Overcast orientation | GSR | A stress/oxidative-response marker used in orientation context |
| Learning | CASK | A neural-development association; not a measured IQ |

The report may summarise a genotype as favourable, average or worth observing. Those words describe a **potential**, not a finished race result. A bird with a favourable endurance genotype can still be poorly conditioned; a bird with an average genotype can still perform well with better management.

**Do not turn a genotype into a guarantee.** Genes set a range of possibilities. Training, health, nutrition, weather, motivation and race conditions decide what is expressed on the day.

## 3. Pathogen section

A pathogen report may show positive/negative results and, for qPCR, a **Ct value**. Ct is the cycle at which the signal crosses the threshold: a lower Ct generally means more target nucleic acid in that sample; a higher Ct generally means less.

A common laboratory convention is:

```text
Ct < 35       positive
Ct 35–40      weak positive / re-test
Ct > 40       negative
```

But **use the cut-off printed on your own report**. Different assays, sample types and laboratories can use different thresholds. A Ct value is not a measure of how sick the bird is, and it is not a substitute for clinical examination.

A negative result means the target was not detected **in that sample at that time**. It does not prove the bird is free of infection. Sampling site, timing, transport and low pathogen load can all produce a false negative. If the bird has symptoms and the result is negative, a veterinary re-test may be more useful than treating the report as a clean bill of health.

## 4. Paternity section

A paternity report usually gives a **paternity index (PI)** for each marker and a **cumulative paternity index (CPI)** across the panel. The CPI is converted into a probability of parentage.

A common reporting convention is:

| Conclusion | Typical threshold |
|---|---|
| Supports parentage | Cumulative probability ≥ 99.99% |
| Excludes parentage | Multiple markers inconsistent with inheritance |

The logic is asymmetric:

- **Exclusion is categorical** — the markers do not fit the claimed parentage.
- **Confirmation is probabilistic** — the result supports the relationship at a very high probability, not a mathematical 100%.

The exact panel, marker count and probability calculation must come from the laboratory that issued the report. Do not compare a CPI from one panel directly with a CPI from another panel without checking that the markers and calculations are comparable.

## 5. Using the report without over-reading it

A practical sequence:

1. **Verify identity.** Ring number, sample ID, date, sample type.
2. **Separate the sections.** A flight-gene result says nothing about infection; a pathogen result says nothing about breeding value.
3. **Read the reference range.** Use the report's own cut-offs and probability statement.
4. **Record the limits.** A negative pathogen result is a point-in-time result; a genotype is a potential, not a performance score.
5. **Decide the next action.** Retain, re-test, pair, or seek veterinary advice — based on the bird's full record, not one page.

For long-term use, link the report to a [gene ID card](/en/blog/gene-id-card-guide/) so the genotype and identity record stay with the bird. For parentage, use the [paternity testing guide](/en/paternity/) and keep the sample chain-of-custody record. For a suspected infection, use the [virus detection service](/en/virus-detection/) and a veterinary assessment.

## Common questions

### Can I ask for a re-test?

Yes. If the sample identity, quality or result is in doubt, ask the laboratory about re-testing. A re-test is most useful when the sample was insufficient, the result conflicts with the clinical picture, or the wrong bird may have been sampled.

### Why can a favourable genotype still produce a poor race result?

Because genotype is one input. Training, health, nutrition, stress, weather and race conditions are separate variables. A report that describes a genotype as favourable is describing potential, not predicting a result.

### What does a negative pathogen result mean?

It means the target was not detected in that sample at that time. It does not exclude infection, especially with poor timing or a low pathogen load. Clinical signs and a veterinary plan should guide the next step.

### Does a 99.99% paternity result prove parentage with absolute certainty?

It supports the relationship at a very high probability. Exclusion is categorical; confirmation is probabilistic. The laboratory's panel and calculation define the exact meaning.

### Is this article veterinary advice?

No. It explains how to read a laboratory report. It does not diagnose or treat a bird. For a sick pigeon, consult a licensed veterinarian.
