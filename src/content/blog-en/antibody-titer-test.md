> **Short answer**: Antibody titer testing answers with data whether vaccination actually worked. For Newcastle disease, the WOAH treats an HI (haemagglutination-inhibition) titre of **log₂ 4 (1:16)** or higher as positive, and field studies treat anything below **log₂ 4** as non-protective — while flock-level immunity only holds when **more than 85% of birds** carry a protective titre.

After vaccination, is the flock actually protected? **Antibody titer testing** answers with data — measuring antibody levels against specific pathogens (Newcastle disease, AI, etc.) to evaluate vaccine efficacy. The uncomfortable truth is that individual immune responses vary enormously: some pigeons develop strong protection while others **fail to respond** entirely. Titer testing is the only way to see that variation instead of assuming the vaccine "worked" because the birds survived the season.

## Why Test Antibodies

Individual immune responses vary widely. Some pigeons develop strong protection while others **fail to respond** due to stress, immunosuppression or poor technique. Antibody testing helps:

- Confirm the vaccine actually worked
- Identify immunity gaps for timely boosting
- Determine optimal booster timing, avoiding over-vaccination
- Build personalized immunization programs
- Catch the "vaccinated but not protected" birds that herd-immunity math depends on

## Methods

| Method | Principle | Features |
|--------|-----------|----------|
| Hemagglutination inhibition (HI) | Antibody inhibits viral hemagglutination | Classic, intuitive, widely used for ND serology |
| ELISA | Antigen-antibody enzyme reaction | High-throughput, standardized, quantitative |

Results are expressed as **titer**; higher titer means stronger protection. The HI titre is the highest serum dilution that still completely inhibits agglutination of a fixed dose of antigen — so "log₂ 4" means a 1:16 dilution, "log₂ 6" means 1:64, and so on. Each step up the scale is a doubling of measurable antibody, which is why a single-step difference is not trivial.

## Reading the Titer — What the Numbers Mean

The thresholds are not arbitrary; they come from the regulatory and field literature on Newcastle disease:

- The **WOAH** treats an HI titre of **log₂ 4 (1:16)** or higher against 4 HAU of antigen as positive ([WOAH Terrestrial Manual](https://www.woah.org/fileadmin/Home/fr/Health_standards/tahm/3.03.14_NEWCASTLE_DIS.pdf)).
- Field studies classify birds with titres of **log₂ 0–2** as low-protection and **log₂ ≥3** as high-protection, and treat titres below **log₂ 4** as non-protective ([van Boven et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2556191/); [backyard poultry study](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7447011/)).
- For a vaccine to be considered immunogenic, a **geometric mean HI titre of ≥6 log₂** 3–4 weeks post-vaccination is the standard for approval in poultry ([potency assessment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6200565/)).

| Titer Level | Immune Status | Action |
|-------------|---------------|--------|
| High (≥ log₂ 4–6) | Good immunity | Keep current program |
| Moderate (log₂ 3, borderline) | Declining protection | Booster |
| Low / negative (< log₂ 3) | Failure or unvaccinated | Booster and re-test |

> Protection thresholds vary by pathogen and method; reports provide reference ranges. The figures above are ND-specific and are the best-documented benchmarks.

## The Herd-Immunity Math Most Lofts Miss

The single most important number in antibody testing is not an individual titre — it is the **>85%** herd-immunity threshold. Experimental transmission studies found that vaccinated birds with low titres can still be protected against *death* yet remain capable of transmitting the virus to flockmates. As a consequence, herd immunity against Newcastle disease "can only be achieved if a high proportion of birds (>85%) have a high antibody titre (log₂ HI ≥3)" ([van Boven et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2556191/)).

This has a concrete consequence: testing only one or two birds tells you almost nothing about the flock. If you sample 10 birds and 3 come back low, you have a flock with a hole in it even though "most" birds look fine. Proportional sampling is not a nicety — it is the difference between measuring immunity and measuring an anecdote.

## Three Key Scenarios

1. **Efficacy evaluation** — sample 2–4 weeks post-vaccination, when antibodies have peaked. Testing too early misjudges because the response is still building.
2. **Booster timing** — boost when antibody drops to borderline. A 4-fold rise in paired sera (two samples taken weeks apart) is the classic sign of *recent infection* rather than just vaccination.
3. **Diagnostic aid** — a 4-fold titre rise in paired sera suggests recent infection, distinguishing a vaccine response from field-virus exposure.

## Sampling & Submission

- Use **blood samples** (wing-vein blood or serum)
- For flock assessment, sample proportionally for better representativeness — the >85% threshold only means something if your sample reflects the flock
- Combine with [pathogen PCR testing](/en/virus-detection/) to distinguish "infection" from "immunity"

> 💡 Tip: Antibody testing and [pathogen PCR](/en/virus-detection/) are two sides of one coin — PCR detects disease, antibodies reflect immunity. Combine both for precise prevention.

## Building an Immunization Program

Antibody testing turns vaccination from a routine into a strategy. By measuring the flock's response after each vaccination, the fancier learns which birds are protected, which failed to respond and when protection is about to fade — then acts on that data rather than on a fixed calendar. This matters most in two places: young birds, whose maternal antibodies can interfere with their first vaccine, and racing birds, whose exposure spikes with every basketing. Sampling proportionally across the flock gives a representative picture, and pairing antibody titers with pathogen PCR separates a healthy immune response from an active infection. The result is precision immunization — boosting only when needed, avoiding over-vaccination, and closing immunity gaps before a pathogen finds them.

## The Author's Take

**Position**: In my view, a fancier who vaccinates without ever running a titre is flying blind — and a fancier who tests one bird and calls it a flock result is flying only slightly less blind.

**Reasoning**: The >85% herd-immunity threshold means protection is a *distribution*, not an average. A single sampled bird tells you about that bird; proportional sampling across the flock is what tells you whether you have a hole. Second, titre testing is the only way to catch the silent non-responders — the birds that got the shot but never seroconverted — before a field challenge finds them for you. Third, the marginal cost of a titre panel is trivial compared to the cost of a single lost race or an outbreak during the season.

**Disclosure**: This is my editorial judgment based on the published transmission and serology literature, not a controlled experiment on racing pigeons specifically. Titre interpretation should always be read against the report's own reference ranges and your veterinarian's guidance.

## FAQ

### How long until results?
Usually 1–3 working days — see [testing services](/en/services/).

### When should I test after vaccination?
Antibodies peak 2–4 weeks post-vaccination; testing then best reflects true response. Testing too early may misjudge because antibodies have not fully developed. For ND, the approval benchmark samples at 3–4 weeks.

### What titre counts as protective for Newcastle disease?
The WOAH treats an HI titre of **log₂ 4 (1:16)** or higher as positive ([WOAH](https://www.woah.org/fileadmin/Home/fr/Health_standards/tahm/3.03.14_NEWCASTLE_DIS.pdf)); field studies treat below **log₂ 4** as non-protective and use **log₂ ≥3** as the high-protection cutoff for transmission modelling ([van Boven et al.](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC2556191/)).

### How many birds do I need to test?
Sample proportionally — the >85% herd-immunity threshold only means something if the sample reflects the flock. Testing one or two birds cannot tell you whether the flock as a whole is protected.

### How does antibody testing differ from pathogen testing?
Antibody reflects immune status (vaccinated/infected); PCR detects current infection. Use both for precise prevention.

### Do young pigeons need testing?
Yes. Testing after a squab's first vaccination verifies whether maternal-antibody interference has faded and immunity has been established, guiding the subsequent immunization program.

### What does a 4-fold rise in titre mean?
A 4-fold rise between paired samples taken weeks apart is the classic laboratory sign of *recent infection* — distinguishing a live field-virus exposure from a stable vaccine response.

### How are samples collected for antibody testing?
Use blood samples — wing-vein blood or serum. For flock-level assessment, sample proportionally for stronger representativeness.

## Key Takeaways

1. Evaluate vaccine efficacy — use antibody titer data to confirm the vaccine worked.
2. Two methods — hemagglutination inhibition (HI) and ELISA, both expressed as titers.
3. ND benchmarks — HI positive at log₂ 4 (1:16); below log₂ 4 non-protective; GMT ≥6 log₂ for vaccine approval.
4. Herd immunity needs >85% of birds at protective titre — test proportionally, not one bird.
5. Three scenarios — efficacy evaluation, booster timing, diagnostic aid (4-fold rise = infection).
6. Complements PCR — PCR detects disease, antibody reflects immunity.
