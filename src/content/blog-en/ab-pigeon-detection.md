# Racing Pigeon AB Pigeon Detection

> **TL;DR**: AB pigeons (substituted birds) are a cheating tactic in pigeon racing — two look-alike birds sharing one ring identity. DNA comparison makes the fraud impossible: a 58-locus profile yields **2⁵⁸ ≈ 2.9 × 10¹⁷ possible combinations**, so a substituted bird's genotype mismatch is caught with certainty on re-sampling.

"AB pigeon" is a hated cheating method — using two similar-looking pigeons to share one identity, one resting while the other races. **AB pigeon detection** uses DNA identification to make substitution impossible.

## What Is an AB Pigeon?

An "AB pigeon" lets multiple birds share the same leg-ring identity:

- Bird A races while Bird B rests
- Frequent swapping keeps the racing bird fresh
- Similar appearance defeats visual checks

The scheme exploits the fact that racing pigeons can look near-identical to the human eye. Because the leg ring — not the bird itself — carries the official identity, swapping a rested double for the racing bird is hard to catch by inspection alone. This is precisely why genetic identity, which lives inside the bird, is the decisive countermeasure.

## How DNA Identification Works

[Sanshi Bio](/en/about/) uses **58 loci** for individual identification:

1. Build a gene profile before the race ([gene ID card](/en/blog/gene-id-card-guide/))
2. Re-sample and test after the race
3. Compare the two genetic profiles
4. Mismatch proves substitution

DNA is each pigeon&rsquo;s inborn, unforgeable identity. No two birds share the same 58-locus profile, so any substitution — however similar the birds look — shows up as a genotype mismatch.

Because a 58-locus profile is generated once and stored, the same archive serves every future verification — a winner can be re-sampled at any time and matched against its original record. This is what makes whole-flock profiling practical for one-loft races: profile every entrant at basketing, and any later substitution is exposed by a simple before-and-after comparison, with no room for the look-alike to slip through.

## Why the Match Is Binary, Not Probabilistic

Unlike a paternity test — which returns a *probability* because a candidate might share some alleles with the bird — identity comparison is a **binary match**. The question is not "how related are these two samples" but "are these the *same* sample." With 58 independently informative loci, the number of possible profiles (2⁵⁸ ≈ 2.9 × 10¹⁷) is so vast that:

- **Same bird** → the re-sample reproduces the identical 58-locus profile → match.
- **Different bird** → even a near-twin cannot reproduce all 58 loci → one or more mismatches → substitution proven.

There is no gray zone. A single discordant locus, confirmed by re-testing, is enough to declare the bird substituted — because a genuine re-sample of the same bird cannot disagree with itself. This is the property that makes AB detection decisive where visual inspection is helpless.

## The Detection Process

1. **Pre-race profiling** — build a 58-locus gene profile for each racing bird
2. **Sample archiving** — retain feather samples on file
3. **Post-race verification** — re-sample and compare the winning birds
4. **Conclusion** — matching loci confirm identity; a mismatch proves substitution

The comparison is objective and binary: either the loci match, confirming it is the same bird, or they do not — there is no gray area for a suspicious-looking double to hide in.

## Running This at Scale: What a One-Loft Race Actually Needs

The promise of AB detection only holds if the logistics are right. Three practical requirements matter more than the science itself:

1. **Profile every entrant at basketing, without exception.** A single unprofiled bird is the hole a cheater exploits. Whole-flock profiling — sampling every entrant at the start of the season — is the standard, and it is why the workflow is feather-based and automated: a technician can collect a roomful of birds in minutes rather than drawing blood from each.
2. **Store the reference profiles and samples securely.** The archive is only as good as its integrity. Reference profiles must survive for future verification, so storage and chain of custody matter as much as the initial test — a lost or swapped reference profile is itself a hole in the system.
3. **Re-sample the winners, not just the suspicious ones.** Substitution is caught by comparing the post-race bird against its pre-race profile. Spot-checking only birds that "look wrong" defeats the purpose, because the entire scheme is designed to look right.

The cost logic is the same as insurance. The per-bird cost of profiling is trivial next to the prize money, stud value or reputation at stake in a high-stakes race. For a one-loft race holding six figures in prize money, profiling the whole field at a few dollars per bird is a rounding error — and it is the only mechanism that makes the result both legally and reputationally bulletproof. The honest framing for organizers: AB detection is not a luxury addition to a big race; it is the difference between a result people believe and a result people argue about.

## What DNA Detection Does Not Cover

Being precise about the limits keeps the tool honest. DNA identity comparison proves **whether the same bird was re-sampled** — it does not, by itself, prove anything else:

- **It does not prove ownership.** The profile says "this is the same pigeon," not "this pigeon belongs to the entrant who claims it." Ownership and eligibility still require paperwork.
- **It does not detect every form of cheating.** Doping, mechanical tampering or illegal treatment are separate problems requiring separate controls; AB detection closes only the substitution loophole.
- **It requires the pre-race reference to exist.** If no baseline profile was built before the race, there is nothing to compare the winner against — which is why pre-season profiling, not post-race testing, is the non-negotiable step.

The right mental model: AB detection is one lock in a multi-lock door. It is the decisive answer to the *substitution* problem, and it is worthless against problems it was never designed to address. A race that treats it as part of a broader integrity program gets the full benefit; a race that expects it to solve every cheating risk will be disappointed.

## FAQ

### Does AB pigeon detection require a pre-race profile?
Yes. Establish a 58-locus profile before the race, then re-sample winners for comparison — only then can substitution be judged.

### How many mismatched loci prove substitution?
Even one confirmed discordant locus is enough, because a genuine re-sample of the same bird cannot disagree with itself. In practice a substituted bird typically mismatches on many loci at once.

### What is the detection process?
Sample and profile racers pre-race, retain the samples, then re-sample the winners post-race and compare genotypes — a mismatch indicates substitution.

### Can leg rings prevent AB pigeons?
Rings can be swapped or duplicated. Genetic identity based on DNA is unforgeable and far more reliable.

### How do one-loft races implement AB pigeon detection?
They are advised to sample and profile all racers uniformly before the season, verify each winner after the race, and combine with [paternity testing](/en/paternity/) to form a complete anti-cheating system.

### What is the difference between AB pigeon detection and a gene ID card?
A gene ID card is a long-term identity profile; AB pigeon detection is the pre/post-race identity verification action. The two complement each other.

### Which events need AB pigeon detection?
High-stakes, one-loft and major races should adopt it, cutting off substitution fraud at the source and preserving the credibility of competition.

### Why is a 58-locus profile decisive against look-alikes?
Two unrelated birds essentially cannot share 58 loci — there are 2⁵⁸ ≈ 2.9 × 10¹⁷ possible profiles — so any substituted bird mismatches on re-testing no matter how similar it looks.

1. AB pigeon is substitution fraud — replacing the racing bird with a same-name, same-ring double.
