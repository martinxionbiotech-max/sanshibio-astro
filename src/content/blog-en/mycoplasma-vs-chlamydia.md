> **Short answer**: Mycoplasma and Chlamydia both cause respiratory and eye signs in racing pigeons that look almost identical, yet differ fundamentally — Mycoplasma lacks a cell wall and is carried by **~9 in 10 racing pigeons**, while Chlamydia is an obligate intracellular organism and a **zoonotic** pathogen that infects humans (psittacosis, incubation **5–14 days**). They cannot be told apart by eye — **confirm the pathogen by PCR first**, then treat accordingly.

In the "respiratory disease" family of racing pigeons, **Mycoplasma** and **Chlamydia** are the two most-mentioned culprits. Fanciers often lump them together under a vague "respiratory problem." In reality they are entirely different pathogens — knowing the difference is the first step to scientific control, and the difference is not subtle: it changes the drug, the human risk, and the transmission strategy.

## Two Pathogens, Fundamentally Different

**Mycoplasma** are prokaryotic microorganisms that **lack a cell wall** — extremely small and able to grow independently. Common pigeon species *Mycoplasma columborale* and *M. columbinum* are frequently blamed for "chronic respiratory disease" (CRD). Crucially, the absence of a cell wall means the β-lactam antibiotics (penicillins and relatives) that work by attacking cell-wall synthesis are useless against them — a clinical detail that explains many "treatment failures."

**Chlamydia** is an **obligate intracellular** organism — it cannot reproduce outside a host cell. The key pigeon species is **Chlamydia psittaci**, also a zoonotic pathogen (it can infect humans).

| Item | Mycoplasma | Chlamydia |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| Nature | Prokaryote, no cell wall | Obligate intracellular |
| Common type | *M. columborale*, *M. columbinum* | *Chlamydia psittaci* |
| Transmission | Vertical + contact | Aerosol/dust inhalation |
| Zoonotic | Generally weak | Yes — psittacosis |
| Drug sensitivity | Confirm by sensitivity testing | Confirm by sensitivity testing; tetracyclines first-line |

## How Common Are They?

- **Mycoplasma** is the background flora of racing pigeons: a Polish survey found **92% of racing pigeons** positive for *Mycoplasma* spp., led by *M. columborale* at **79%** ([Polish study](https://doi.org/10.1637/12018-122718-reg.1)).
- **Chlamydia psittaci** is rarer in an individual loft but has been documented in **467 bird species**, is present in at least **17 genotypes**, and is estimated to cause about **1% of community-acquired pneumonia** in humans ([PMC review](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11622591/)).

The practical asymmetry: Mycoplasma is everywhere but usually mild; Chlamydia is less ubiquitous but carries a human-health consequence Mycoplasma does not.

## Overlapping and Diverging Symptoms

The two share heavily overlapping signs, which is why "mycoplasma vs chlamydia in pigeons" is so hard to judge by eye:

| Site | Mycoplasma | Chlamydia |
|------|-----------|-----------|
| Respiratory | Sneezing, head-shaking, moist rales, open-mouth breathing | Nasal discharge, abnormal breathing, dry cough |
| Eyes | Swollen eyelids, tearing | Conjunctivitis, tearing, discharge |
| General | Depression, poor appetite, weight loss | Lethargy, wasting, poor breeding performance |
| Character | Protracted, recurrent, silent carriers | Recurrent; aerosol-spread |

The two are almost indistinguishable in "tearing, sneezing and dullness" — Mycoplasma leans chronic, Chlamydia more contagious and a human health risk.

## Why You Cannot Judge by Symptoms Alone

Because symptoms overlap, "looks like mycoplasma" does not equal "is mycoplasma." Blindly treating by experience carries two risks:

1. **Treating in the wrong direction**: the two respond differently to medication, and guessing may be completely ineffective. The no-cell-wall biology of Mycoplasma means a penicillin-family antibiotic is wasted on it, while Chlamydia needs intracellular penetration (tetracyclines).
2. **Ignoring the zoonotic risk**: if it is actually Chlamydia, a keeper cleaning the loft unprotected may inhale the pathogen and harm their own health.

Confirmation is the only reliable route. See the [pathogen encyclopedia](https://pathogens.sanshibio.net/) for side-by-side Mycoplasma and Chlamydia entries.

## A Scientific Response: Confirm First, Then Act

1. **Sample and test**: when respiratory signs appear, run pathogen detection first to identify the culprit.
2. **Veterinary guidance**: after confirmation, a veterinarian prescribes by pathogen type and **sensitivity testing** — never self-medicate.
3. **Isolate and ventilate**: respiratory disease spreads by aerosol, so isolate sick birds, ventilate and lower density.
4. **Breeder purification**: Mycoplasma can transmit vertically, so PCR-screen breeders before breeding to build a negative population.
5. **Personal protection**: when Chlamydia is suspected, wear a mask and gloves while cleaning the loft and wash hands afterward.

## The Author's Take

**Position**: In my view, the highest-value distinction a fancier can learn is not "which one is worse" but "which one can hurt *me*" — because Chlamydia is the zoonotic one, and treating it as "just another respiratory bug" while cleaning the loft bare-faced is a self-inflicted risk no bird symptom justifies.

**Reasoning**: First, the biology forces different drugs — Mycoplasma's missing cell wall and Chlamydia's intracellular niche mean a guessed antibiotic is often a wasted antibiotic. Second, the zoonotic asymmetry is concrete: *C. psittaci* infects humans via inhaled dust with a 5–14 day incubation, while pigeon Mycoplasma species are not a meaningful human threat. Third, the prevalence asymmetry (Mycoplasma in 9 of 10 birds) means a "respiratory problem" is *usually* Mycoplasma — but "usually" is exactly when complacency about Chlamydia bites. PCR resolves the guess cheaply.

## FAQ

### What exactly is the difference between mycoplasma and chlamydia in pigeons?
The essential difference is biology: Mycoplasma has no cell wall and grows independently, while Chlamydia must reproduce inside host cells. Symptoms look nearly identical — both show respiratory and eye problems — so PCR is needed to tell them apart. This biology also drives treatment: cell-wall-targeting antibiotics are useless against Mycoplasma, and Chlamydia needs intracellular-penetrating drugs.

### Which is more serious, Mycoplasma or Chlamydia?
There is no simple "worse." Mycoplasma is protracted, recurrent and vertically transmissible, affecting breeders and found in ~92% of racing pigeons; Chlamydia is highly contagious and zoonotic, with a 5–14 day human incubation and ~1% of CAP. Both should be tested promptly.

### Does one-eye tearing always mean Chlamydia?
Not necessarily. Mycoplasma, Chlamydia and other irritants can all cause one-eye tearing. A single "one-eye cold" symptom cannot identify the pathogen — testing is required.

### Are Mycoplasma and Chlamydia mixed infections common?
Yes. Respiratory disease often involves two or more pathogens, so a [multiplex panel](/en/blog/qpcr-principle/) is recommended — clarifying the mixed infection is the only way to respond precisely.

### Can Chlamydia infection spread to humans?
Yes. *Chlamydia psittaci* can infect humans through aerosols, causing fever, headache and respiratory signs with a **5–14 day** incubation ([CDC](https://www.cdc.gov/psittacosis/about/index.html)). Wear a mask and gloves when cleaning the loft and wash hands afterward.

### What should I watch when medicating after confirmation?
Always medicate under veterinary guidance, choose a direction by **sensitivity testing**, use the full course, and re-test after treatment to confirm clearance and avoid resistance.

## Key Takeaways

1. Mycoplasma lacks a cell wall, while Chlamydia is obligately intracellular — fundamentally different pathogens, different drugs.
2. Their respiratory and eye signs overlap heavily and cannot be distinguished by eye.
3. Mycoplasma is ubiquitous (~92% of racing pigeons) but usually mild; Chlamydia is zoonotic and can infect humans through aerosols.
4. PCR precisely separates Mycoplasma from Chlamydia, and multiplex panels reveal mixed infections.
5. Confirm by testing first, then treat under veterinary guidance — the only scientific path.
