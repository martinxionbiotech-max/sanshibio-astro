**Short answer:** The credibility of a DNA report is partly decided before the sample reaches the laboratory. A rejection usually means insufficient material, degraded DNA, contamination, unclear identity or unsuitable transport — not that the laboratory is being difficult. Treat sampling as part of the test, not as an errand before it.

## 1. What the laboratory actually needs

The laboratory does not need “a feather” or “some blood” as objects. It needs enough intact, clean DNA that can be attributed to one bird.

Sample quality therefore has four dimensions:

1. **Quantity** — enough DNA for the test.
2. **Integrity** — DNA not degraded.
3. **Purity** — no inhibitors or contaminating DNA.
4. **Traceability** — the sample can be tied to the correct bird.

If any one fails, the result may be unreliable.

## 2. The reasoning: why a sample that “looks fine” can fail

### A feather is not automatically a DNA sample

Feather testing normally needs pulp or root tissue. An old feather, a feather with no follicle tissue, or one exposed to heat and moisture may yield too little or degraded DNA.

### Blood has anticoagulant and storage requirements

The wrong tube, repeated freeze-thaw cycles or a long transit time can damage DNA. Dried blood cards have their own drying and storage requirements.

### Swabs can pick up environmental DNA

Oral, cloacal or environmental swabs can carry feed, faeces, feathers or DNA from another bird. Swab material and preservation matter.

### A labelling error can be worse than degradation

A perfect sample with an unclear label, a lost tag or a shared ID is not a “low-quality sample”; it is a result that cannot be attributed. In paternity work and high-value transactions, that is more serious than low DNA yield.

### Transport can change the sample

Heat, humidity, pressure, leakage and delay can turn a suitable sample into an unsuitable one. The chain matters from collection to receipt.

## 3. What follows: when to re-sample

| Situation | Possible consequence | Action |
|---|---|---|
| No follicle, too little or old feather | Insufficient DNA | Collect a fresh feather |
| Wrong blood tube or repeated freeze-thaw | Degraded DNA | Re-collect or use a suitable card |
| Swab contaminated with environment | False positive/indeterminate | Re-sample with clean technique |
| Unclear label or duplicate ID | Result cannot be attributed | Re-label and re-sample |
| Heat-damaged or leaking shipment | Sample failure | Assess and re-sample |
| Report ID does not match the bird | Cannot be used for trade/registration | Stop and audit the chain |

## 4. Editorial take

I would rather have a laboratory reject a sample than “make it work” and issue a report that looks complete but cannot be trusted.

- **Rejection is a quality-control outcome**, not a service failure.
- **Re-sampling is usually cheaper** than a wrong report in a transaction.
- **Chain of custody is not paperwork**; it is what makes the report usable in a sale, auction or dispute.

For a high-value bird, I would record who collected the sample, when, how it was stored, who transported it and who received it — more carefully than the report itself. The report is one page; the chain is the reason it can be believed.

## 5. Minimum pre-submission checklist

- Confirm the sample type and the laboratory's requirements.
- Use fresh, sufficient material attributable to the target bird.
- Label each bird separately with a waterproof, secure ID.
- Avoid cross-contamination: change gloves, clean tools, separate packaging.
- Follow the laboratory's storage and shipping instructions; do not trade speed for heat or delay.
- Keep collection photos, ID records and the courier tracking number.

## 6. Boundaries

Different laboratories and tests have different sample requirements. This is a general explanation, not a substitute for the receiving laboratory's instructions. The laboratory makes the final acceptance decision.
