# Sample Collection Guide for Racing Pigeons

> **TL;DR**: Half of a reliable result depends on proper sampling. The three sample types map to three purposes — **feather for genes, swab for pathogens, blood for antibodies** — and each has its own storage rule: feathers dry at room temperature, swabs and blood chilled at **2–8°C**, all shipped ideally within **48 hours**.

Half of a reliable test result depends on the lab; the other half depends on **proper sampling**. Errors in collection, storage or shipping can fail a test or distort results. This guide covers three common sample types and how to handle each one correctly.

## Three Sample Types

Each sample type serves a different testing purpose, so choosing the right one is the first decision:

| Sample | Collection Site | Used For |
|--------|-----------------|----------|
| Feather | Wing feather root with pulp | Gene testing, gene ID, paternity |
| Swab | Oral/pharyngeal, cloacal | Virus, mycoplasma, pathogen testing |
| Blood | Wing vein | Antibody testing, immunity |

Getting this mapping wrong is a common and avoidable failure. A swab cannot substitute for a feather in a gene test (it collects too little host DNA), and a feather cannot substitute for a swab in pathogen detection (pathogens concentrate at the mucosal surface, not in the feather pulp). Match the sample to the question before you touch the bird.

## 1. Feather Sampling (Gene Testing)

1. **Select** 3–5 wing or tail feathers with intact **root pulp** (whitish translucent part)
2. **Pluck** gently along the growth direction, avoid breaking
3. **Store** in the provided sampling bag/tube, label the bird
4. **Note**: squabs can use down feathers; avoid the molting period when feather quality is poor

> Feather pulp is the "DNA bank" — ensure the pulp stays intact when plucking. A single intact follicle yields roughly 10–100 ng of DNA, several times what a PCR needs, but only if the pulp survives the pluck.

## 2. Swab Sampling (Pathogen Testing)

1. **Tool**: use dedicated **flocked swabs**, not cotton tips
2. **Oral swab**: insert to the back of the mouth and throat, rotate 3–5 times
3. **Cloacal swab**: insert 1–2 cm, rotate to collect mucosal secretions
4. **Store**: snap the swab into a sterile tube with preservation solution

Flocked swabs release more collected material into the assay than ordinary cotton tips, which is why they are standard for pathogen detection. The difference is physical: flocked swabs have a brush-like tip of perpendicular fibres that traps material on the surface and releases it efficiently into the assay, whereas cotton binds the sample and holds much of it back — so a cotton tip can quietly turn a real positive into a weak or false negative.

The site also matters. Respiratory signs call for an oral/pharyngeal swab; digestive signs call for a cloacal swab. When in doubt, submit both — the marginal cost is small and the coverage doubles.

## 3. Blood Sampling (Antibody Testing)

1. Collect 0.3–0.5 mL from the wing vein by experienced personnel
2. Separate serum and refrigerate, or ship anticoagulated whole blood
3. Avoid hemolysis; use strictly sterile equipment

Blood is the one sample that genuinely benefits from experienced hands — a botched venipuncture stresses the bird and can hemolyze the sample, and hemolyzed serum interferes with antibody read-outs. If you are not confident drawing blood, delegate this one and use feather/swab self-collection for the rest.

## Storage & Shipping

| Aspect | Requirement |
|--------|-------------|
| Temperature | Feathers at room temp; swabs/blood at 2–8°C |
| Time | Ship promptly, ideally within 48 hours |
| Labeling | Clearly mark bird ID and test item |
| Packaging | Leak-proof, crush-proof, with test form |

Temperature handling matters because DNA in dry feather pulp is stable at room temperature, whereas swab and blood samples contain live or labile material that degrades without refrigeration. Getting this wrong is one of the most common causes of failed or unreliable results, so always match the storage condition to the sample type before you ship.

The 48-hour rule exists for a reason: every hour a swab sits warm, viral nucleic acid degrades and the Ct value climbs, pushing a real positive toward the gray zone. Feathers are more forgiving because dry DNA is stable — but "more forgiving" is not "indefinite," and a sample that sits for weeks invites re-testing.

## The Five-Step Submission Process

To keep every batch running smoothly, follow these five steps:

1. **Book and register** — confirm the test items and sample requirements with the laboratory first
2. **Sample properly** — collect and label samples per the standards above
3. **Fill the submission form** — note the loft, bird information and test items
4. **Ship the samples** — pack and send according to the storage requirements
5. **Receive the report** — the laboratory issues the report on its regular turnaround once samples arrive

> 💡 Tip: For batch submissions, confirm sample volume and shipping method with the lab in advance. See [testing services](/en/services/).

## The Seven Ways Samples Go Wrong

Most failed or ambiguous results trace back to one of a small set of sampling errors. Knowing the list is the cheapest insurance in testing, because every item is avoidable:

1. **Wrong sample for the question** — a swab sent for a gene test, or a feather sent for pathogen detection. Match the sample to the test first.
2. **Shed instead of plucked feathers** — the desiccated follicle on a molted feather yields almost no DNA. Pluck fresh feathers with intact pulp.
3. **Cotton tips instead of flocked swabs** — cotton holds the sample back and can turn a real positive weak.
4. **Warm swabs** — pathogen nucleic acid degrades without refrigeration; ship swabs and blood chilled at 2–8°C.
5. **Pooled samples** — mixing birds makes the result un-attributable; one bird per tube, always.
6. **Unlabeled or mislabeled samples** — a perfect test on a mystery tube is worthless; label bird ID and test item at collection.
7. **Sitting too long before shipping** — the 48-hour rule exists because every extra warm hour degrades the sample.

The common thread is that none of these are "lab problems" — they are all decided at the loft, before the sample ever leaves your hands. A loft that treats collection as a disciplined routine, rather than an afterthought, will simply see fewer re-tests and fewer ambiguous reports than one that does not.

## Choosing the Sample by Symptom

When you are unsure which sample to collect, let the clinical picture decide. The mapping is straightforward and worth memorizing:

| What you see | Sample to collect | Why |
|--------------|-------------------|-----|
| Respiratory signs (sneezing, breathing) | Oral/pharyngeal swab | Respiratory pathogens concentrate in the upper tract |
| Digestive signs (diarrhea, crop issues) | Cloacal swab | Enteric pathogens shed in droppings |
| No symptoms — gene/identity/paternity question | Breast feathers | Feather pulp carries the host's own DNA |
| Vaccination or immunity question | Blood (serum) | Antibodies live in the blood, not the feather or swab |

When signs are mixed or unclear, submit **both** an oral and a cloacal swab — the marginal cost is small and the coverage doubles. The one mistake to avoid is guessing: a feather cannot answer a pathogen question and a swab cannot answer a gene question, so when in doubt, ask the laboratory *before* you collect rather than sending a sample that cannot answer what you actually want to know.

## FAQ

### Is one feather enough for gene testing?
A single feather with intact pulp usually suffices, but 3–5 are recommended in case of missing pulp or contamination. One intact follicle carries roughly 10–100 ng of DNA.

### Does swab sampling hurt the pigeon?
No, when done correctly. Oral/cloacal swabs collect only surface secretions; be gentle.

### Why can't I use an ordinary cotton tip for a swab?
Flocked swabs have a brush-like tip that traps material on the surface and releases it efficiently into the assay; cotton absorbs and holds the sample back, which can turn a real positive into a weak or false negative.

### Do I need to fast the pigeon before sampling?
No fasting needed for feather (gene) testing; for pathogen testing, sample before medication to avoid interference. Blood sampling has no strict fasting requirement either.

### Can I pool multiple birds into one sample?
No. Each sample must be labeled and tested individually — pooling makes results impossible to attribute.

### What if my samples get lost in shipping?
Use a tracked courier and keep the tracking number. For important samples, consider splitting shipments or arranging on-site collection with the laboratory.

### Any tips for batch submissions?
Confirm sample volume and shipping method with the lab beforehand, label every sample with its number, and attach a complete submission form to avoid omissions or mix-ups.

## Key Takeaways

1. Three sample types — feather for genes, swab for pathogens, blood for antibodies.
2. Feather pulp is essential — the DNA source for gene testing (10–100 ng per follicle).
3. Flocked swabs beat cotton tips — higher release, better accuracy.
4. Refrigerate swabs and blood — 2-8°C, ship within 48 hours.
5. Label clearly — mark each sample with bird ID and test item.
